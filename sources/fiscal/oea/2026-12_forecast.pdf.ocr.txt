Executive Summary
December 2026 — Forecast Little Changed
The broad contours of the economic outlook remain largely intact as the Office of Economic
Analysis (OEA) updates forecasts in quarter six of the biennium. The dominant themes remain
resilient economic activity with the possibility of some moderate acceleration through the end of
the biennium—a forecast profile which was previously characterized as resembling a hockey
stick. These conditions are fostering a parallel resilience on the inflation landscape. The economy
continues to demonstrate mid-cycle characteristics, whereby the probability of a near-term
recession remains little changed from the previous assessment (near 18%).
The latest data on national gross domestic product showed not only stubborn resilience in the
face of headwinds including rising interest rates and mounting energy costs, but also some
compelling signs of acceleration. Topline inflation-adjusted growth of 3.4% in Q3 2026 marked the
fastest pace in a year—and handily exceeded the average over the last five years. The underlying
composition of activity showed impressive domestic demand from both businesses and
households. Consumer spending is outpacing overall growth, while business investment is
surging at a pace that is exceptional relative to recent history.
State level economic activity has lagged the national trend in the current economic cycle, in part
due to restructuring in the semiconductor sector. However, a narrowing gap between the national
and state unemployment rates at a time when both are trending lower is an encouraging signal
that this laggard tendency may be on the verge of catching up. The degree to which this gap
narrows will have significant implications for the revenue forecast heading into the 2027-2029
biennium.
For the past several quarters, diminished economic confidence has resulted in muted underlying
activity in the labor market. Businesses have been hesitant to adjust staffing levels and
employees have lacked confidence to pursue new opportunities. The net result has been a low
fire-low hire malaise at both the national and state level.
In OEA’s prior forecast update, a potential incremental improvement of this stasis was
acknowledged, and recent labor reports corroborate progress toward more favorable conditions.
The pace of hiring has improved in recent months, the unemployment rate is trending downward,
layoff announcements remain low and job openings/postings suggest labor demand is improving.
The unfortunate consequence of resilient economic and labor conditions is that overheated
inflation conditions have similarly proved to be entrenched. The inflation landscape is fraught with
5

a multitude of factors contributing to elevated price pressures at present. Energy prices remain a
dominant factor, but they are far from being the sole contributor, as tariffs, labor costs, upstream
(or wholesale) price pressures and a more inflationary mindset among businesses and
households are also adding to collective price pressures.
A sustained improvement in labor conditions will give policymakers the latitude to focus more
squarely on the persistent inflation overshoot through tighter monetary policy. The duration and
magnitude of the inflation overshoot—and lack of realized progress in recent months—all suggest
that monetary conditions are insufficiently restrictive. As a result, economic forecasters broadly
expect the Federal Reserve to raise interest rates to achieve a more appropriate policy stance.
Presently, the consensus expectation is for one or two more quarter-point rate increases. The
resilience of economic activity and price pressures in response to these hikes will determine if
further tightening becomes warranted. Elevated inflation and the policy efforts to restrain these
price pressures will be a dominant theme for the foreseeable future.
Each fall sees tax returns filed with an extension arrive by October 15th. These returns are
disproportionately higher income and more complex compared to their early season
counterparts. For tax year 2025, these returns have modified our understanding of wealth effects
that began the prior year. Capital gains, dividend, and IRA income continue to grow at double-digit
rates as financial and equity markets soar to new heights. This healthier-than-expected income
growth can be seen feeding through the personal income tax forecast for Q4, even as the
remaining aspects of the forecast remain little changed. The growth inflection is manifesting as
previously predicted, supporting historically normal gains outside of the wealth components.
The Q4 forecast for General Fund revenues is only marginally changed at $11 million above the
prior forecast. Personal and corporate income taxes are modestly revised due to factors
described above and a healthier corporate profits outlook. The increase in income taxes is offset
by adjustments to estate taxes and interest earnings. The projected ending balance in the General
Fund is now $411 million, still $62 million below the Close-of-Session level.
Beyond the General Fund, modest revisions are also evident. Lottery earnings are $12 million
higher than previously expected, almost entirely realized in the latest quarterly transfer.
Corporate activity taxes have increased $15 million from Q3, driven mostly by lower-thanexpected refunds during the fall filing season. Finally, marijuana taxes are slightly revised $1
million below the prior forecast, a mere 0.4% adjustment for the biennium.

Economic Outlook
Macroeconomic Setting
Economy looks to be mid-cycle
As measured based on a calendar day count, one could erroneously presume the current
economic expansion—now in its sixth year—is approaching its later stages based solely on the
typical length of an economic cycle over the last several decades. However, the longevity of an
expansion is far more accurately assessed based on fundamentals than a simple calendar count
of months or quarters.
From a fundamentals-based perspective, the current state of the economic cycle bears greater
resemblance to the middle-stages of an expansion rather than one gasping on the verge of
contraction. To be sure, cycle-ending risks are present, such as stretched financial market
valuations, elevated inflation pressures and high energy costs, but there is little reason to believe
the ultimate reconciliation of these imbalances looms imminently. Rather, falling unemployment,
a robust corporate profit trend and improving domestic demand are key underpinnings of this
mid-cycle judgement. In fact, it is entirely plausible that the current expansion will extend through
(and possibly beyond) the 2027-29 biennium.
The national economy has displayed remarkable resilience in the face of several headwinds and
shocks. Partly by design and partly due to luck, some economic buffers have bolstered activity at
what could have been potentially critical junctures. For example, tax cuts (by design) occurred
concurrently with the implementation of tariffs. More recently, tariff refunds following the US
Supreme Court ruling (fortuitously) came at about the same time that rising energy costs due to
the Iran war were buffeting the economy.
In just the last few months, consumers (in aggregate) appear to have taken a second runup in
energy prices mostly in stride as well, due to an apparent willingness to at least temporarily draw
down savings. As gasoline prices revisited their 2026 highs and diesel prices soared well beyond
levels visited in the spring, inflation-adjusted consumer spending grew at a pace last exceeded in
the final quarter of 2024. This spending momentum was not supported by income growth, which
barely edged out inflation during this period. Instead, it was possible due to a draw-down of
consumer savings, which fell by more than 20% from a year earlier.

7

Dissaving is not a sustainable strategy to support consumption, but it looks to have successfully
bridged a gap which is now being supported by improving labor conditions. Despite low readings
on consumer sentiment, households’ actions signaled greater confidence that economic
conditions were poised to pick up. This confidence now appears to have been well-placed as the
latest labor data show impressive strengthening.
The national jobs report for October showed hiring gains (+165,000) well above forecasters’
expectations; additionally, the report also showed sizeable upward revisions to the prior two
months (+84,000). The pace of hiring appears to be demonstrating a durable reacceleration since
the start of the year, and the gains increasingly look to be broad-based across industries. The
three-month average nonfarm payroll increase stood at 137,000 jobs per month as of October
compared to 61,000 as of January. (In the same period one year ago, the three-month average was
declining at a rate of 45,000 jobs lost per month.)
The pace of job creation has been sufficient to absorb labor market slack and in turn push
unemployment lower—to 4.1% compared to 4.5% a year earlier. Diminished labor slack is helping
to support wages at a time when flaring consumer prices are significantly impairing real income
growth.
An accelerating and broadening hiring trend combined with relatively firm wage pressures means
that aggregate employment income (i.e. total wage and salary income) is starting to reaccelerate.
If this pattern is sustained, it will enable households to reverse the dissaving trend, in turn
supporting faster household consumption.

Consumer spending is the single-largest and most important engine of growth for the national
economy, but it is only part of the robust domestic demand landscape at present. Business
investment in areas such as equipment, software and other intellectual property has been
8

growing at a double-digit pace for the last several quarters. Demand for data centers and AIrelated investment have been a major catalyst of this investment surge, but the trend appears to
be broadening into more conventional categories, as well.
Historically, rising industrial capacity utilization, expanding corporate profits and supportive
capital markets have all been essential driving factors of business investment. Despite some
recent financial market turbulence, these factors appear to remain largely intact. These are
typical characteristics of the middle stages of an expansion, whereby businesses have both the
ability and need to increase capacity.
The latest data on corporate profits reveals an impressive acceleration underway. The national
accounts show corporate profits expanding roughly 5% in year-on-year terms from late 2023 to
mid-2025. At the start of this year the pace had improved to 10%, and as of mid-year it was 21%. A
robust earnings and profit environment provides businesses the ability (and confidence) to hire
and invest.
Similarly, the need for business fixed investment is evident in rising industrial capacity utilization.
The fact that overall utilization rates have risen appreciably serves as an indication that business
investment needs are spreading beyond AI/datacenters and adjacent sectors. As shown in the
accompanying chart below, capacity utilization currently stands near a 33-month high.
In response to the aforementioned factors, the national accounts show business fixed investment
growing 12% over the past year, which is double the pace during the previous economic cycle.

While topline economic growth is showing a relatively stable growth profile, rising 2.1% over the
last year, the surge in demand for building materials and capital equipment is masking the true
degree to which domestic demand is strengthening. The demands of the business investment
surge are being met through inventory drawdowns and rising imports, both of which subtract from
overall GDP. It is not uncommon for a pickup in business investment to be camouflaged by these
other offsetting factors. For this reason, a narrower measure of output reflecting household and
business demand provides a clearer perspective on underlying domestic demand, which is likely
to set the stage for the growth outlook in 2027. The core message from these results is that the
domestic economy is likely on a firmer trajectory at present than the headline numbers would
otherwise imply.

Stagnation averted, but at a cost
Economic resilience in recent quarters appears to have fortified inflation pressures, which
accelerated through midyear and have now run above policymakers’ target for over five years.
Inflation drains purchasing power of consumers and businesses, but it has recently begun to have
an augmented impact on financial markets by contributing to higher interest rates. This occurs
through two primary channels: Market-determined interest rates rise as investors demand a
higher inflation premium and short-term rates increase in anticipation of a more restrictive
monetary policy response from the Federal Reserve. While the former initially appeared to be the
dominant driver, recent signaling from monetary policymakers suggests Fed officials are
preparing to act more forcefully, as well.
The outlook for inflation under the economic conditions described above will pose a challenge for
policymakers. A higher cost of financing consumption and investment poses a material risk to the
medium-term outlook, particularly for interest-sensitive sectors such as autos and housing.
10

Several economic cycles have been ended through some combination of unconstrained inflation
and the Federal Reserve’s monetary response.
The current elevated pace of inflation is not purely the result of tariffs, energy prices and other
one-time shocks (although these are certainly contributing factors). The contributing factors are
more broad-based, including high input prices, firm industrial pricing power and stubbornly high
employment costs. The economy has simply not cooled off sufficiently and for long enough from
its post-pandemic surge to temper excessive price pressures.

In conclusion, the Office of Economic Analysis views the current economic cycle to be in the
middle stages. As such, it is entirely possible that the expansion endures through the end of the
current biennium, and it is plausible that it could stretch beyond the 2027-29 biennium as well.
Recession probabilities are relatively low at present, despite risks from persistent inflation and
lofty financial market valuations. The baseline economic view assumes that a strengthening
growth outlook, driven by burgeoning domestic demand from both households and businesses,
will support an improved outlook for state output and employment over the medium term.

Oregon’s Economic Outlook
In prior forecast iterations, the Office of Economic Analysis has described Oregon’s growth
outlook as resembling a hockey stick: slower growth in the first part of the current biennium,
broadly consistent with last year’s performance, followed by an inflection point whereby stronger
growth emerges in the later stages of the biennium. That previously anticipated inflection now
appears to have materialized, and the characterization of the forecast profile remains
appropriate. If forecast expectations for stronger growth in 2027 are realized, trends in state
output and employment should continue to firm as a result.

Many of the headwinds responsible for increasing recession probabilities in the past few quarters
have moderated in recent months, therefore leaving the probability of a near-term recession little
changed from the prior iteration. An additional reason for this maintenance is the resilience both
the U.S. economy and Oregon have continued to exhibit in the face of unpredictable factors. The
resilience of the labor market has remained a reoccurring theme highlighted by this office.

The state has been plagued by year-over-year job losses for the greater part of 2026; however, the
most recent September jobs report posted a positive yearly job gain for the first time since early
2025. Over this period of continuous year-over-year job losses (January to July), the
unemployment rate in Oregon remained unchanged at 5.2%. This suggests that the necessary
number of new jobs to maintain steady unemployment, or the breakeven employment growth
rate, has changed significantly from recent history. Slower population growth and an aging
population have contributed to this changing labor market dynamic.
The state unemployment rate has recently ended this stasis. After remaining at 5.2% for seven
months, the rate has gradually started to decline over the past two months and now currently sits
at 5.0%. This coincides with two consecutive months of positive job gains.
With monthly job growth in 2026 fluctuating between negative and positive, the data suggest that
breakeven employment growth is now likely near zero. The average monthly job gain of 4,200 jobs
over the last two months (August and September) was enough to meaningfully reduce the
unemployment rate, which helps explain why the unemployment rate remained unchanged
despite some months of positive job gains. Should monthly job gains continue through the rest of
the year at a similar pace, the unemployment rate should continue to decline.
The recent drop in layoff announcements provide another encouraging indication for Oregon’s
labor market. The Worker Adjustment and Retraining Notification’s (WARN) have declined
12

noticeably from their peak in 2025, suggesting that the low fire dynamic appears to hold true and
provides some evidence of continued labor market resilience in the state.
At the same time, state economic growth, which had largely caught up to the U.S. in the first
quarter, appears to have diverged temporarily in the second quarter of this year. The Q2 GDP
release shows Oregon trailing the nation by just over one percentage point. For most of the
pandemic recovery Oregon has lagged the national economy. Therefore, it is not unusual at this
moment to see the state maintain its slight underperformance to national trends in indicators
such as unemployment. However, this pattern differs from what Oregon previously experienced
before the pandemic, as the state consistently outpaced national GDP growth for four
consecutive years between the two most recent recessions of 2008 and 2020.
As shown in the chart below, Oregon's year-over-year real GDP growth was 1.1% in Q2 2026,
compared with 2.2% nationally. However, the combination of a falling unemployment rate and
recent employment gains indicate that state-level economic output is likely to continue firming
throughout the rest of 2026.

Employment flickers
In September, data published by the Oregon Employment Department (OED) showed that
nonfarm payroll employment rose by 2,600 jobs over the past 12 months. This is the first yearover-year job gain the state has registered since February 2025. In prior forecast iterations,
13

consistent job losses were reported. To be sure, one data point does not make a new trend,
however the recent positive job report alongside the declining unemployment rate is an
encouraging signal for Oregon’s labor market.

Within the state, one sector has consistently stood out from the rest. The private education and
health services industry continues to act as the state’s primary driver of job creation, which is a
pattern that mirrors national trends. This is in part due to the country’s aging demographics and
the need for more health services. Over the past 12 months, the industry has added 13,400 jobs,
or 3.8%. This outpaces any other industry in Oregon with positive job gains over the year, as
shown in the chart below.

Although September was the first month with positive year-over-year job growth, there have been
five months thus far into 2026 that have experienced a positive monthly job gain. Most
interestingly, August and September both added an average of 4,200 jobs, which coincided with
the first declines in the unemployment rate. The other three months of positive monthly job gains
(April to June) averaged nearly 1,500 jobs, which was not enough to move the unemployment rate.
Altogether, the first seven months of 2026 had an average monthly job loss of roughly 1,200 jobs,
yet the unemployment rate remained unchanged over this period.

The job losses through most of the year, alongside the stabilizing and slowly declining
unemployment rate, indicate that the labor market may be transitioning to a lower underlying
growth trend. This is also the case for the U.S. economy, as migration patterns continue to shift
and the population continues to age.

14

Recent positive job growth and a declining unemployment rate have contributed to the state’s
somewhat improved near-term economic outlook compared to the prior forecast iteration. The
narrowing gap between Oregon and the U.S. unemployment rates, coupled with recent
employment gains, provides an encouraging signal that state level economic activity should
improve. Despite this, there remains some risk for the employment outlook as job gains have
been concentrated in a limited number of industries, particularly healthcare and social
assistance. A continuing decline in Affordable Care Act enrollment and cuts to Medicaid could
result in a pause of the growth of health care employment that has supported much of the growth
for Oregon and the U.S. economy.

While unemployment rate changes are a valuable barometer of labor market conditions, looking
at the length of time that individuals remain unemployed adds an additional layer of
understanding. The unemployment benefit exhaustion rate measures the share of Oregonians
who have received their maximum allotment of UI benefits (following 26 weeks). An elevated
exhaustion rate is an indicator of labor market fragility and difficulties for displaced workers to
find new sources of employment.

To date, the Oregon unemployment
insurance benefit exhaustion rate has
risen to a level reminiscent of the first
half of 2024. Although the benefit
exhaustion rate seems to have ticked up
through 2025 and 2026 thus far, the rate
is still low by historical standards. This is
an indication that the labor market has
slowed but remains functional. This
echoes the “low hire-low fire”
characterization that many labor
economists have continued to apply to
their assessment of national conditions
and has been characterized in prior

Climate Change: Carbon accumulation in the atmosphere and associated warming

Some factors which could mark a critical break from the baseline forecast could come from a
substantial rise in labor productivity (defined as output per hour worked) and an increasing labor

levels of output, all else equal. While the labor force participation rate is not particularly
contingent on higher population growth and net in-migration, it can be an important contributor to
higher employment levels. Oregon has experienced periods where employment growth has
exceeded population growth, mainly driven by changes in the labor force participation rate.
Pandemic migration patterns differ substantially from recent history. There is good reason to think
some of those changes will remain in the decade ahead, particularly when it comes to the
combination of housing affordability and working from home resulting in lower migration to
Oregon than in decades past. However, such a slow growth baseline does leave upside risks.

ahead? By 2035, Oregon’s employment and total personal income are roughly 70,000 and 3.3%
higher than in the baseline forecast, respectively.

Revenue Outlook
Recent personal income tax (PIT) returns, associated with the extension filing season, reveal
stronger-than-previously-believed wealth gains. Sources of income in this category include
dividends, interest earnings, capital gains, and IRA distributions. Capital gains income looks to
have grown upwards of 25% in tax year 2025. Similarly, IRA distributions may have matched tax
year 2024’s 20% growth. With equity and financial market indicators exhibiting continued strength
in 2026, these upward revisions feed through to the entirety of the PIT revenue forecast. The
effects are magnified by the fact that nearly 85% of wealth income is earned by the top quintile
(20%). This means that, relative to the more equitably distributed wage and pension income, a
disproportionate share of wealth income is taxed at the top 9% and 9.9% rates. Beyond this
driving factor, the growth inflection discussed in previous forecasts is shaping up much as
expected. This is reflected in revenue tracking, which has adhered close to the Q3 forecast when
aggregated across the major revenue streams. As such, the forecast is minimally changed, except
for PIT in future biennia.

Personal Income Tax
As noted previously, late season returns filed with an extension are redefining our understanding
of income composition and strength for tax year 2025. Even as the associated tax receipts are
known much earlier, information regarding income components like capital gains and IRA
distributions can alter expectations for future tax years due to the incidence of these components
among the highest income earners. For example, capital gains income is almost entirely earned
by the top quintile (20%), and as such is tax predominantly at the top 9% and 9.9% rates. When
the growth of these “wealth” types of income is stronger than previously believed, it raises both
the level of future income and the effective rate dictating the tax that results. This impact can be
seen in the entirety of the personal income tax forecast as a level shift up.

higher. The effects of House Resolution 1, offset somewhat by state legislation (Senate Bill 1507

Tax Law Assumptions
The revenue forecast is tied to current state law. After each legislative session, OEA incorporates
adjustments to the revenue forecast produced by the Legislative Revenue Office. As each year
passes and the effects of law become evident in the data, adjustments are phased out. However,
many tax laws have sunsets, or end dates, built into them. These demarcations must be
maintained such that the impact of extending the laws can be newly incorporated in turn.
Complication arises when considering the effects of federal law on Oregon revenues. Due to the
assumptions built into these national forecasts and how national data enter the revenue models;
it would be logistically difficult to maintain a current federal law revenue forecast. In addition,
federal laws often have imminent sunsets and are frequently extended. Adhering to a current
federal law forecast would result in large and unnecessary annual revisions. For this reason, the
revenue forecast assumes the most reasonable outcome for federal law.

Population and Demographic Outlook
Population and Demographic Summary
Based on the most recent decennial census, Oregon’s resident population on April 1, 2020, was
4.237 million. During the past decade, Oregon gained 406,182 residents or 10.6%. This decennial
gain was the second lowest since the first census count in Oregon in 1860 after gaining
statehood. Still, the gain was substantial enough to yield one additional congressional seat for the
state. Oregon now has a total of six members in the House of Representatives. This is a rare
occurrence as it took 40 years for Oregon to gain one additional seat.
Oregon’s population growth of 10.6% over the 2010-20 decade was the 11th highest in the nation,
excluding Washington D.C. The growth rate for the decade lagged all of Oregon’s neighboring
states except California. Oregon’s growth has experienced some turbulence since the 2020
census and the corresponding COVID-19 pandemic. At OEA, the PSU Population Research Center
(PRC)’s recent postcensal estimate is used as the base for the office’s population forecasts. The
PRC’s estimates show a loss of population between 2020 and 2021. Such a loss was very unusual
for Oregon because this happened after nearly four decades. Since then, the recovery in
population growth has been very slow.
The estimates show Oregon’s population growth has remained at a low level not seen since the
mid-1980s, indicating timid economic recovery in the post-pandemic years. As the strict federal
immigration policy continues to be enforced and Oregon’s high-tech employment shows signs of
stress, population growth will be affected accordingly. Oregon’s population growth is expected to
show a slow positive increase in the future reaching 4.491 million in the year 2035 with an average
annual rate of growth of 0.4% between 2025 and 2035.

Working-age adults move to Oregon as long as there are favorable economic and social
conditions such as: job opportunities, affordable housing and childcare, a good educational
environment, personal safety, and a better quality of life that projects real and perceived positivity
about the state. As a result of a sudden rise in the number of deaths and a drop in the number of
births coinciding with the COVID-19 pandemic, the natural increase (number of births minus
number of deaths) turned negative starting in the year 2020 and will continue through 2035 and
beyond.
Migration will be solely responsible for Oregon’s future population growth. Without a positive net
migration stream, Oregon’s population will start a steady decline. Under a few scenarios, the
negative natural increase may shrink or reverse itself. Such reversal can happen if women start to
have more children due to behavioral or motivational factors, improved life expectancy leading to
fewer deaths, or a large net in-migration of young-age adults contributing to an increase in the
number of births. For decades, Oregon’s population growth was well above the national average.
However, due to the sluggish economy and employment situation, Oregon’s population growth
has been lagging well below the national average since 2021.

Older Population (65+)
The overall older population (65+) was growing at a relatively slow pace during the late 1990s and
early 2000s when the depression era birth cohort entered this age group. The 65+ population
picked up a faster pace of growth once the baby-boom cohort started maturing into the older age
group. This age cohort has hit the plateau of high growth rates exceeding 4.0% annually between
2011 and 2018. The group will experience a high but diminishing rate of growth in the coming years
as all those in the baby boom cohort complete the transition. The average annual growth of the
65+ population will be 1.6% during the 2025-2035 forecast period, which is nearly four times the
rate of growth of the state’s overall population.
As a sign of the massive demographic structural change of Oregon’s population, the population of
people 65+ has exceeded the number of children under the age of 18 since 2023. To illustrate the
contrast, in 2000 the older population numbered a little over half of the number of children in
Oregon, and now they outnumber the children. This is due to the decline in the fertility rate,
improvement in the life expectancy, as well as the aging of the large baby boom population.

The oldest working age population, aged 45-64, has also seen a dramatic demographic upheaval

Procedure and Assumptions
Population forecasts by age and sex are developed using the cohort-component projection
procedure. The population by single year of age and sex is projected based on the specific
assumptions of vital events and migrations. The projection procedure entails the model that
"survives" the initial population distribution by age and sex to the next higher age-sex category in
the following year and then applies age-sex-specific birth and migration rates to the mid-period
population.
The population by single age-sex detail from the 2020 census modified for age heaping and the
most recent estimated total population for Oregon by Population Research Center of Portland
State University are the base for the forecast. The numbers of births and deaths through June 30,
2025, are from Oregon's Center for Health Statistics. All other numbers and age-sex detail are
generated by OEA.
Annual numbers of births are determined from the age-specific fertility rates projected based on
Oregon's past trends and past and projected national trends. Oregon's total fertility rate is
assumed to remain close to 1.4 children per woman. This rate is well below the replacement level
fertility of 2.1 children per woman during their reproductive life. Currently, all the states in the
union are experiencing below replacement fertility levels. Such a low fertility rate means the state
will experience population decline in the long run unless fertility rate improves, along with strong
net positive migration to compensate for the loss due to the excess of deaths over births.
Life Table survival rates are developed for the year 2020. Male and female life expectancies for the
2020-2035 period are projected based on the past three decades of trends and national projected
life expectancies. After a sudden decline during the COVID pandemic, improvements in life
expectancies are expected over the forecast period. At the same time, the difference between the
male and female life expectancies will continue to shrink in the long run. The male life expectancy
at birth was 77.3 and the female life expectancy was 81.8 in 2010. Because of the COVID-19
pandemic, the number of deaths suddenly increased, and the actual life expectancies declined.
The life expectancy at birth in 2020 was 76.9 and 81.7 years for males and females, respectively.
This is expected to improve to 80.6 years for men and 85.2 years for women by 2035.
Estimates of the number of net migrations are based on the residuals from the difference between
population change and natural increase (births minus deaths). OEA’s migration forecast is done
separately and considers employment change, unemployment rates, income/wage of Oregon and
neighboring states and the nation, and Oregon’s past population change and migration trends.
Distribution of migrants by age and sex is based on detailed data from the American Community
49

Survey. The role of net migration in Oregon’s population growth has gained prominence as the
natural increase has turned negative and the only way to grow Oregon’s population is to bring in
more people from other states/countries than those who leave the state. The net migration
numbers in this forecast cycle are lower primarily due to a lower employment forecast in the long
run. Between 2025 and 2035, net migration is expected to be in the range of 17,100 to 26,000,
averaging 24,700 persons annually with net migration rates ranging between 4.0 to 6.1 per
thousand population.
