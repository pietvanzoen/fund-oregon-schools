Introductions
Chair Mooney welcomed commissioners, staff and opened the meeting. ODE leadership
provided updates on agency priorities, including support efforts for the Bend-La Pine
community following the recent tragedy. Staff also shared information about recent State Board
actions, including adoption of instructional time rulemaking and high-quality instructional
material rules. Commissioners were encouraged to remain aware of ongoing implementation
efforts and support ODE is providing to school districts statewide.

Quality Education Model Overview
Staff continued the Commission’s onboarding process with a comprehensive overview of the
Quality Education Model (QEM), its statutory foundation, and its role in estimating the
resources necessary to support Oregon’s educational goals. Discussion focused on the
Commission’s responsibility to identify funding levels necessary to provide a quality education
and how the QEM serves as an educational adequacy benchmark rather than a school funding
formula.
Commissioners reviewed the history and development of the model and discussed how it has
evolved over time while maintaining its core purpose and structure. Staff explained the
distinction between maintaining current educational services and identifying the resources
needed to fully implement the practices outlined in the Quality Education Model. The group
also reviewed common misconceptions about the model, including its relationship to the State
Fund and legislative budgeting process.

Understanding the Model and Oregon’s Funding System
Staff provided a deeper look at how the Quality Education Model is constructed using prototype
elementary, middle and high schools to estimate statewide educational costs. Commissioners
discussed the various factors included in the model, including staffing, instructional supports,
class size assumptions, transportation, technology, operational expenses, and employee
benefits.

The Commission also discussed changes in Oregon’s K-12 funding structure and the growing use
of targeted grant programs alongside the State School Fund. Members noted the complexity of
multiple funding streams and the challenges associated with understanding how resources,
programs, and outcomes connect at both the state and district levels. Discussion highlighted the
importance of funding transparency and the need for commissioners to understand the broader
education finance landscape as they prepare for future report development.

Policy Context and Future Commission Work
Commissioners discussed the increasing legislative interest in educational funding adequacy,
accountability, transparency, and student outcomes. Staff described how educational research,
professional judgement, and best practices contribute to the ongoing evaluation of the model
and inform future recommendations.
The group also explored issues likely to influence future Commission work, including changes to
financial reporting systems, efforts to align funding with outcomes, and possible refinements to
the model’s methodology. Staff introduced alternative approaches to educational cost modeling
and discussed the tradeoffs between simplicity, accuracy, transparency, and comparability.

Next Steps
Chair Mooney encouraged commissioners to continue documenting questions and topics for
future discussion. Staff will continue the onboarding process in upcoming meetings with
additional information on accountability systems, educational funding structures, and Quality
Education Model assumptions. The next meeting will future support commissioners'
understanding of the model, its role in informing Oregon’s education policy discussions and
include staff presentations regarding the Education Accountability Act.

Wrap Up
Chair Mooney concluded and adjourned the meeting.

QEC Legislative Establishment and Requirements
The Quality Education Commission was established in 2001 in ORS 327.497 and 327.500:
“The Quality Education Commission should be established to define the costs sufficient to meet the established quality goals for
kindergarten through grade 12 public education.”
ORS 327.506:
(2) Each biennium the Quality Education Commission shall determine the amount of moneys sufficient to ensure that the state’s system
of kindergarten through grade 12 public education meets the quality goals.
(3) In determining the amount of moneys sufficient to meet the quality goals, the commission shall identify best practices that lead to
high student performance and the costs of implementing those best practices in the state’s kindergarten through grade 12 public schools.
Those best practices shall be based on research, data, professional judgment and public values.
(4) Prior to August 1 of each even-numbered year, the commission shall issue a report to the Governor and the Legislative Assembly that
identifies:
(a) Current practices in the state’s system of kindergarten through grade 12 public education, the costs of continuing those
practices and the expected student performance under those practices; and
(b) The best practices for meeting the quality goals, the costs of implementing the best practices and the expected student
performance under the best practices.
(5) In addition, the commission shall provide in the report issued under subsection (4) of this section at least two alternatives for meeting
the quality goals. The alternatives may use different approaches for meeting the quality goals or use a phased implementation of best
practices for meeting the quality goals.

Ethics and Conflict of Interest
●
●

Oregon Government Ethics Commission (OGEC)
Oregon Government Ethics Law Guide for Public Officials (2026)
○ Pg 35: Conflicts of interest
○ A conflict of interest occurs when a public official makes a decision or
recommendation, or takes an official action that would (actual conflict of interest) or
could (potential conflict of interest) financially impact, positively or negatively, any
one or more of the following:
■ The public official
■ A relative of the public official
■ A member of the public official's household
■ A business associated with the public official
■ A business associated with the public official’s relatives or household members
It is important to note that the types of conflicts of interest addressed in Ethics Law
are limited to financial conflicts of interest. They do not address issues such as bias
or favoritism.
16

Ethics and Conflict of Interest (continued)
●
●

Prohibited Use of Office or Position (pg 62)
Ethics Law prohibits public officials from using or attempting to use their office or position to
obtain a financial benefit or to avoid a financial harm (together referred to as a financial impact),
if the financial impact would not otherwise be available but for the office or position they hold.
The prohibited use of office or position provisions apply to financial impacts on:
○ the public official, the public official’s relatives, and the public official’s household members
○ any businesses that the public official, their relatives, or their household members are
associated with
The following are some examples of a public official’s prohibited use or attempted use of their
office or position:
○ A state employee uses a government vehicle to take their children to soccer practice.
○ A school superintendent signs a contract between the school district and the company that
employs the superintendent’s spouse.
○ A volunteer firefighter lets their roommate do their laundry at the fire station.
○ A city councilor pressures staff to change a proposed ordinance to increase the value of real
estate that the city councilor owns

17

Ethics and Conflict of Interest (continued)
●
●

Gifts (pg 94)
Ethics Law limits the value of gifts a public official can accept from certain people.
If the source of a gift has a legislative or administrative interest in the actions of the public official, the
public official can only accept gifts from that source with a total value of $50 or less in a calendar year.
However, if the source of the gift does not have a legislative or administrative interest, there is no limit on
the value of the gift or gifts that the public official can accept.
The restrictions on the value of gifts from a source with a legislative or administrative interest include gifts
given to public officials, their relatives, or members of their household. To decide if a gift can be accepted
with or without limit, the public official must know the source of the gift. Anonymous gifts cannot be
accepted. The burden of knowing whether a gift can be accepted rests on the individual public official.
In analyzing whether an offer of a thing of value can be accepted, you want to ask four questions. We will
analyze each question below.
○ Does the thing of value being offered qualify as a gift under the Ethics Law?
○ Is the gift being offered by a source that has given the public official other gifts valued in excess of
$50 in aggregate (including the value of the gift being offered) during the calendar year?
○ Could the source of the gift be reasonably known to have a legislative or administrative interest in
the public official’s decisions or votes?
○ Do any of the gift exceptions in ORS 244.020(7)(b) apply?

Ethics and Conflict of Interest (continued)
●
●

Nepotism (pg 114)
Generally, public officials may not participate in personnel actions, including interviews,
discussions, or debates by their public body when the personnel action involves their
relatives or household members. Personnel actions include:
○ Appointments
○ Employment
○ Promotions
○ Discharges
○ Firings
○ Demotions
This prohibition includes when the relative or household member is seeking to become a
member of a governing body over which the public official exercises jurisdiction or
control, even if that member would serve in an unpaid capacity. For example, if the
Mayor’s spouse was seeking an appointment to a position on the City’s budget
committee, the Mayor could not participate in actions related to their appointment
including interviews, discussions, or debates.

Quality Education Model

Purpose and Statutory Intent
Statutory Baseline (ORS 327.497–327.506): Codifies the Quality Education Commission
(QEC) to determine the level of funding required to meet state performance goals.
Adequacy Benchmarking: Establishes a research-grounded target for public K–12
education, serving as an analytical tool rather than a budget execution formula.
Independent Evaluation: Designed to enable the legislature to evaluate whether state
education appropriations are sufficient to support high-quality educational services
across Oregon.
Focus on Statutory Goals: Directly connects state funding estimates to statutory
educational targets, including student achievement standards and high school
completion.

46

Constitutional Anchor (Article VIII, Section 8)
Constitutional Requirement: Oregon’s Constitution explicitly requires the Legislative
Assembly to appropriate funds sufficient to provide a quality education system.
Mandated Biennial Reporting: If state funding falls below the QEM adequacy target,
the Legislature must issue a formal report explaining why appropriations are
insufficient.
The QEM's Analytical Role: Acts as the objective standard and technical evidence
base used by lawmakers to evaluate constitutional funding sufficiency.
Legislative Accountability: Provides a transparent benchmark connecting revenue
decisions to their real-world impact on educational services.
47

Model Overview
Bottom-Up Cost Modeling: Calculates educational service costs from the ground up
by identifying baseline activities, staffing, and operational inputs required to deliver
quality instruction.
Full-System Scope: Evaluates total state public education resources—not strictly
those distributed through the State School Fund (SSF).
Evidence-Based Design: Incorporates educational research, best practice surveys, and
demographic adjustments to establish resource requirements.
Dynamic Recalibration: Updated biennially to reflect shifts in student demographics,
labor costs, PERS rates, and operational inflation.
48

Dual Fiscal Framework:
• Baseline Cost of Service Delivery: Establishes the cost required to maintain
current operational activities across Oregon public schools under existing
economic conditions.
• Full QEM Target: Projects the comprehensive investment required to fully
implement research-backed educational best practices and achieve state quality
goals.
• Quantifying the Adequacy Gap: Allows the legislature to evaluate the variance
between Legislative Adopted Budgets, baseline operational costs, and the full
QEM target.
• Policy Application: Allows budget-writers to distinguish between simply
maintaining status-quo operations and actively funding systemic quality goals.

QEM Architecture: The Prototype School Approach
• Standardized Educational Prototypes: Constructs statewide cost estimates using
three baseline school profiles:
• Elementary School Prototype: 360 students
• Middle School Prototype: 500 students
• High School Prototype: 1,000 students
• Resource Scaling: Maps licensed teachers, classified staff, specialists,
administrators, and materials directly to prototype student counts.
• Analytical Aggregation: Scales prototype resource models upward to build the
statewide K–12 adequacy estimate.
51

Core QEM Cost Drivers
Class Size & Staffing Ratios: Defines target class sizes and instructional staffing
requirements across elementary, middle, and high school levels.
Instructional Time: Models the cost of school year length, summer learning, and
professional development days for educators.
Specialized Student Supports: Factors in dedicated staffing for English learners,
special education, alternative education, and mental health resources.
Operational Inputs & Benefits: Incorporates compensation schedules, PERS
employer contribution rates, health insurance, instructional technology, and
facility overhead.
52

Myth/Fact: QEM Purpose & Scope
Myth: The QEM is a budget execution or allocation tool that determines local school
district spending or sets the State School Fund formula.
Fact: The QEM is an adequacy benchmarking tool. It sets a statewide funding
standard so the Legislature can evaluate proposed budgets against quality goals,
leaving actual local spending decisions to school boards.

Myth: The QEM models only funds distributed through the State School Fund.
Fact: The QEM evaluates all state public education resources and programs, capturing
the total state investment required for K–12 service delivery.
53

Myth/Fact: Costing Mechanics vs. DAS CSL
Myth: The QEM uses top-down expenditure tracking and standard inflation to
calculate a State School Fund Current Service Level (CSL).
Fact: The QEM is a bottom-up resource model that covers all state K–12 resources;
not just the State School Fund. Rather than applying top-down inflation to historical
budgets, it models the actual cost of baseline educational activities, adjusting for
specific shifts in enrollment, input costs, and economic factors.

54

Myth/Fact: Target Stability & Scope Expansion
Myth: The QEC arbitrarily adds new components to the model over time to
continuously push the adequacy target higher.

Fact: For most of the past 25 years, the full QEM adequacy target remained below
what the original 1999 full QEM estimate would have been if simply adjusted for
standard inflation and enrollment growth. Rather than expanding scope, modern
adjustments reflect structural cost shifts (such as rising benefit rates, operational
overhead, and specialized student supports) that have outpaced general inflation.

55

QEM History
Post-Measure 5 Tax Shifts (1990s): Passage of Measure 5 (1990) and Measures 47/50
(1996–1997) shifted K–12 funding responsibility to state general revenues, creating a need
for a statewide adequacy benchmark.
1999 Model Inception: Developed under Speaker Lynn Lundquist to establish a bottom-up
cost estimate for quality education, moving policy away from funding solely based on
available historical revenues.
Kitzhaber/Bunn Interim Commission (1999–2000): Refined initial model assumptions and
developed a phased strategy for state implementation.
Statutory Codification (2001): HB 2295 formally created the independent Quality
Education Commission (ORS 327.497–327.506) to maintain the model and report
biennially.

25 Years of QEM Findings: Adequacy Gap Trends
Persistent Funding Gap: Over 25 years of reporting consistently demonstrate a variance
between actual legislative appropriations and the full QEM target.
Economic Cycle Vulnerability: The adequacy gap expands sharply during state economic
downturns (e.g., 2001, 2008) and contracts during periods of revenue expansion.
Impact of Structural Costs: Rising non-discretionary costs, particularly PERS employer
rates and healthcare premiums, have historically consumed revenue gains intended for
QEM quality enhancements.
Longitudinal Benchmark Value: Provides a multi-decade historical record documenting
the relationship between state investment levels and public education funding needs.

Evolution of QEM Best Practices & Target Goals
Shift Beyond Basic Inputs: Evolved from modeling basic staffing ratios toward
incorporating research-backed educational best practices.
Targeted Equity Practices: Integrated evidence-based interventions for multilingual
learners, historically underserved populations, and high-poverty schools.
Expanded Outcome Metrics: Expanded model targets to incorporate early literacy,
career-technical education (CTE), and mental/behavioral health supports.
Regular Best Practices Surveys: Refined through periodic statewide surveys of
Oregon educators and administrators to ground model assumptions in current
classroom realities.

Core Policy Tensions
Adequacy vs. Allocation: Statewide adequacy benchmarks evaluate systemic need, while the SSF
formula distributes available funds. Generating granular district-level adequacy estimates risks
blurring the line between statewide benchmarking and local entitlement expectations.
Precision vs. Stability: Highly differentiated models offer greater demographic precision but create
target volatility that complicates multi-year district planning. Stable models preserve longitudinal
comparability across biennia.
Transparency vs. Complexity: Professional judgment panels clearly show non-technical audiences
specific classroom inputs; statistical cost functions provide analytical depth through complex
econometric formulas.
Granularity vs. Responsiveness: Detailed cost-function updates proposed on 8-year cycles may
prove less responsive to real-time policy shifts than traditional biennial QEM updates.
