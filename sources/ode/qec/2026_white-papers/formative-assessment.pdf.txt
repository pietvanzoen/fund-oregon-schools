Executive Summary
The formative assessment process is one of the most powerful, research-affirmed levers
available to improve student learning. Unlike tests, often high-stake events, formative
assessment is a continuous, student-centered practice that is embedded in daily instruction. It
enables teachers and students to use evidence of learning in real time to adjust actions,
promote agency and accelerate learning. This white paper outlines why formative assessment is
essential to Oregon’s vision for equitable, ambitious instruction. It identifies the system
strengths and challenges that shape its implementation and presents a statewide strategy to
scale high quality formative assessment as a central component in every classroom.

The Case for Formative Assessment
Decades of research demonstrate that formative assessment yields the greatest positive impact
of any documented instructional practice on student learning. It works by transforming the
learning experience from traditional one-way instruction into a more dynamic partnership
between students and teachers. Students are clearer about what they are learning, where they
are in their progress and what actions they will need to take to help them grow. Teachers gain
insight into student thinking and can respond immediately with targeted feedback, in the
moment adjustments to produce deeper engagement.
Oregon’s balanced assessment system positions formative assessment as the key component to
student achievement. Yet misunderstandings persist. Too often, “formative assessment” is
mistaken for events like quizzes or exit tickets. This narrow view does not fully capture the real
purpose or power of formative assessment. It is a process, not an event. It relies on
instructional practices, student discourse, meaningful feedback and tasks aligned to the full
depth of the standards.

Oregon’s Framework: The Four-Leaf Clover Model
Oregon uses the Smarter Balanced Four-Leaf Clover model to illustrate formative assessment as
an interconnected cycle:
1. Clarify learning goals and success criteria: Students and teachers share a vision of what
success looks like, grounded in high-quality instructional materials (HQIM) and gradelevel standards.
2. Elicit evidence of student learning: Evidence emerges through questioning, discussion,
tasks, demonstrations and dialogue. Not for evaluation but for understanding.
3. Interpret evidence: Teachers and students analyze what the evidence reveals about
strengths, opportunities for growth and potential next steps.

4. Act: Feedback, instructional adjustments, self-assessment and peer feedback accelerate

When consistently used, formative assessment promotes partnership between educators and
students, builds student agency, promotes equity and promotes rigor across all classrooms. It
positions assessment as a process that honors student identity, experiences and assets as
essential components of learning.

System Strengths and Needs
Oregon has foundational strengths that support high-quality formative assessment, including
adoption of HQIM, clear academic standards and an emerging culture of ambitious teaching
and statewide expertise in balanced assessment. Still, there are challenges.
Challenges
•

Misalignment between standards and assessment rigor: Evidence gathered in
classrooms does not always reach the Depth of Knowledge (DOK) levels required by the
stands, limiting its usefulness.

•

Inconsistent coherence across the system: At times, curriculum, instruction and
assessment operate in silos, leading to mixed expectations for rigor and uneven student
experiences.

•

Insufficient time, tools and professional learning: Teachers need protected
collaboration time, modeling, coaching and shared frameworks to implement formative
assessment deeply and consistently.

Uneven understanding of asset-based practice: When misunderstood, asset-based
instruction can result in lowered expectations rather than strengthened rigor that builds
on student knowledge and strengths.
Opportunities
•

•

Strengthen coherence from classroom to system level.

•

Link HQIM, formative assessment practices, and ambitious teaching as a unified
approach to equity.

•

Use evidence to support continuous improvement at classroom, school and district
levels.

•

Collaborative professionalism: Professional learning communities, observation protocols
(such as FARROP) and shared frameworks improve consistency and expertise statewide.

every student experiences learning that is responsive, challenging and meaningful. By scaling
formative assessment as a statewide practice, Oregon strengthens its commitment to
education equity and sets the conditions for all students to learn and grow.

Introduction
Assessment is often thought of as a series of big, high-stakes events such as standardized tests
or final exams that educators have little control over because instruction must be paused to
create time and space to practice how to engage with it and then time to administer it. Yet this
narrow view overlooks the form of assessment that students and teachers engage with most
frequently: the formative assessment process. Unlike one-time measures, the formative
assessment process is woven into daily instruction, providing ongoing opportunities for
feedback, adjustment, and growth. When practiced effectively, it has the potential to be truly
transformational, shifting learning from a static evaluation to a dynamic process. More than any
other type of assessment, formative practices can profoundly shape student learning
experiences, fostering deeper understanding, greater ownership, and lasting impact. 1
Formative assessment, also known as assessment for learning, is a cornerstone of effective
classroom practice. High-quality formative assessment depends on a respectful, collaborative
classroom environment where teachers and students work in partnership. When implemented
well, students become active partners in their learning, and teachers have many opportunities
to respond to students’ needs. It is one of the most flexible and powerful tools educators can
use to make meaningful differences for students. The formative assessment process functions
as a process of educator-to-student and, more importantly, student-to-student interactions and
behaviors that increase student ownership of the learning process. The goal is to improve
understanding of intended learning outcomes while fostering students’ growth as self-directed
learners.
In 2021, the Michigan Assessment Consortium updated the definition originally developed in
2006 by the Formative Assessment for Students and Teachers (FAST) group of the Council of
Chief State School Officers (CCSSO).
“Formative assessment is a planned, ongoing process used by all students and educators
during learning and teaching to elicit and use evidence of student learning to improve
student understanding of intended disciplinary learning outcomes and support students
to become self-directed learners. Effective use of the formative assessment process
requires students and educators to integrate and embed the following practices in a
collaborative and respectful classroom environment:
• Clarifying learning goals and success criteria within a broader progression of learning;
• Eliciting and analyzing evidence of student thinking;

The Four-Leaf Clover Model of Formative
Assessment in Oregon
Oregon uses the four-leaf clover from Smarter Balanced to illustrate
the formative assessment process with each leaf symbolizing one
essential dimension of practice. This visual metaphor emphasizes that
formative assessment is not a single event, but a continuous,
interconnected cycle that supports student learning. 9
•

•

•

Dimension 1: Clarify Learning Goals and Criteria for Success
At the top left, the process begins with clarification. This step
is directly tied to high-quality instructional materials (HQIM),
where teachers establish clear learning goals and success criteria. It is also the moment
when educators draw upon what they already know about their students—their cultural
backgrounds and strengths—to design activities and tools that honor and build upon
those assets. Learning goals are written in student-friendly language beginning with, “I
understand” or “I can”. Clarification ensures that both teachers and students share a
common understanding of what success looks like.
Dimension 2: Elicit Evidence of Student Learning
The second dimension focuses on gathering evidence. Here, both educators and
students actively elicit information about learning using multiple sources of evidence
such as questioning, discussion, performance tasks, or other strategies to draw accurate
conclusions about student learning. This evidence is not collected for evaluation, but to
provide insight into how students are progressing toward the established goals.
Dimension 3: Interpret Evidence to Determine Next Steps
Once evidence is gathered, the third dimension involves interpretation. Teachers and
students work together to analyze information, often “on the fly” from multiple points
of instruction, identifying patterns, strengths, and areas for growth. This step is critical
because it views evidence not as one singular event, but part of an ongoing process that
turns data into meaningful insights that guide instructional decisions. Educators must
anticipate student thinking as part of their planning process.

Proposed Solutions: Benefits and Impact
Formative assessment benefits both educators and students by creating a classroom culture
grounded in high expectations and mutual belief in every learner’s potential. When educators
work collaboratively with students and set ambitious academic goals, they foster an
environment where belonging and confidence thrive. As Anthony Muhammad emphasizes,
“Students will learn more and be more successful in an environment where all educators believe
they can learn at high levels and those educators work together to convince the students that
they can achieve lofty academic goals teachers set out for them.” 14 At the same time, the
nature of the feedback matters. When feedback is done well, it instills responsibility and
ownership to learning and contributes to collaboration and a culture of safety. Ron Berger
writes that feedback must be descriptive to make an impact. They are distinguished by these
features:
•
•
•
•
•

Individualized and growth-oriented: Feedback should target the improvement of a
specific piece of work, skill, or disposition for an individual student or small group.
Private and relational: Exchanges typically occur between teacher and student or
among peers, not as public commentary for the whole class.
Grounded in trust: Feedback is most effective within a long-term, constructive
relationship that prioritizes respect and support.
Positive and strategic: Students are sensitive to personal feedback; constructive,
encouraging comments lead to better outcomes than criticism.
Informed and tailored: Effective feedback draws on deep knowledge of the student,
their strengths, current stage of growth, and what will spark the next step forward.15

This shared commitment, paired with formative practices and commitment to improving,
ensures that assessment is not merely a measure of learning but a driver of equity,
engagement, and growth.

Benefits for Students
Inviting students to engage in self-assessment and peer feedback is one of the most powerful
ways to position them as true partners in their own learning. These practices empower
students to examine evidence of their progress, identify where they currently stand, and
determine the next steps in their learning journey. Through reflection, students build
ownership of their learning and develop critical metacognitive skills that extend beyond the
classroom.
Peer feedback helps students develop a clear understanding of success criteria by analyzing
peer work in relation to learning goals. When guided by strong protocols and models like the
example provided through Austin’s Butterfly, students learn how to give and receive feedback
constructively, using exemplars and structured routines to ensure their insights are meaningful
and actionable. Self-assessment complements this process by empowering students to use
evidence from their own learning to decide next steps. It motivates them by fostering agency
and ownership, while also deepening their understanding of success criteria. Both practices are
most effective when supported by clear structures that ground reflection in rigorous standards.
The outcomes of these practices extend far beyond individual assignments. Students gain
empowered agency, learning to rely on evidence of their own progress to chart future learning.
They experience inclusivity and belonging, as student-centered approaches honor lived
experiences and integrate diverse funds of knowledge into the foundation of learning. Most
importantly, they engage in high-quality learning experiences, becoming active partners in the
teaching and learning process while tackling content with rigor.
Together, self-assessment and peer feedback transform classroom routines into intentional
practices that drive equity, agency, and deeper learning—ensuring students are not only
participants in education but co-creators of their own success.

Benefits for Teachers
Formative assessment practices support an asset–based orientation, enabling teachers to
integrate students’ existing knowledge and culture into the design of learning goals and tasks.
An asset-based approach helps teachers build on what students already know when setting
learning goals and success criteria. It also draws on students’ experiences and strengths when
designing learning tasks, while maintaining high expectations, so students are both challenged
and supported to succeed.
At times, asset orientation is misunderstood as lowering rigor to help students feel successful.
This misconception assumes that drawing on students’ strengths means simplifying the work or

assessment honors students’ strengths while opening doors to the depth of learning they
deserve.

Needed Resources
Clarify: Determine Learning Goals & Develop Success Criteria
Clear learning goals and success criteria help everyone know what success
looks like. Success criteria should be tied directly to the learning goals and
written in words students can easily understand. Because these goals
connect to standards, both teachers and students know not only what is being learned but also
how challenging the work will be. With this clarity, teachers can take practical steps to move
learning forward.
It’s important to think about the level of challenge students face. If students only work at Depth of
Knowledge (DOK) Levels 1 and 2—where they recall facts or show basic understanding—they miss
chances to grow as thinkers. When students are given opportunities to work at DOK Levels 3 and 4, they
can:
•

Level 3: Apply ideas in new situations, showing flexible thinking.

Level 4: Analyze and combine information from different sources, building strong critical

Elicit: High-Quality Tools (Assessment and HQIM)
While DOK 1 items serve a purpose, it is strongly encouraged that educators also provide
opportunities at DOK Levels 3 and 4. These higher-level tasks allow students to demonstrate

How well they connect their ideas to the ideas of others;

•

Whether they ask questions that push thinking further; or

•

How they notice and respond to different perspectives.

When students see clear examples of what these skills look like in practice, they can better
recognize evidence of their own learning and decide on the next steps to take. This reflection
goes beyond simply “joining in.” It helps students build on peers’ ideas, make connections
between concepts, support their thinking with evidence, and consider viewpoints that may
differ from their own.
Because skills like collaboration, reasoning, and critical analysis are valuable in Math, Science,
Language Arts, and Social Studies, success criteria framed in this way help students develop
habits of mind that transfer across subjects. These habits strengthen learning and empower
students to use their strengths in new and challenging situations.

coherent system where teaching, learning, and assessment are all working toward the same
goals.

Conclusion
The Formative Assessment process is more than a classroom strategy. It is a powerful practice
that helps students and teachers grow together. By using the formative assessment process,
educators gain daily insights into student learning and can adjust instruction in real time. At the
same time, students’ voices and ideas are elevated, giving them greater ownership of their
learning. When the formative assessment process is at the center of teaching, instruction
becomes responsive, equitable, and aligned with high standards.
This process empowers students to engage more deeply, think critically, and take charge of
their progress. For educators, it provides clear evidence to guide decisions and ensure that
every learner has access to rigorous opportunities.
Without these supports, formative assessment may be uneven or surface-level. With them, it
can become a universal practice across the state, a foundation for coherence in teaching and a
driver of equity and excellence in student learning.
Oregon has the chance to lead by example. By funding and building the structures needed to
scale formative assessment, the state can ensure that every student experiences learning that
