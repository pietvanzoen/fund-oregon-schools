Introduction
School funding has been at or near
the center of public policy debate in
Oregon for decades, and the sources
and distribution of revenue have
evolved accordingly. Taxes have been
created, adjusted and capped for
various policy reasons, from protecting
taxpayers to serving students with
unique needs. The burden of funding
schools has shifted from district
taxpayers to the state, which also
distributes billions of dollars in federal
funds every year according to strict
rules. Much funding, meanwhile, is
distributed according to a formula that
weights poverty, English language
proficiency and several other factors.

ABOUT THE AUTHOR

Jim Scherzinger retired in 2016
following nearly 40 years of public
service in Oregon. During his career,
he spent 20 years in the Legislative
Revenue Office, 14 of those as
director. Scherzinger also worked
for Portland Public Schools, first as
chief financial officer and then as
superintendent. He subsequently
worked in various roles within the
Oregon Department of Human
Services, including chief financial
officer, interim deputy director
and chief operations officer.

With all these changes, it’s time to take
a big-picture look at the system, how
we got here, and where we should
go next. This paper is a first step
toward that very large task. After a little
context setting, this paper attempts to
describe the public sources Oregon
uses to fund early childhood and K-12
education operations, how those
resources are distributed through the
system and why it was set up that way.

Context
Public schools in the U.S. are run by state
and local governments. The first schools
were local, dating back to the 17th century.
The local nature was preserved by the 10th
Amendment to the U.S. Constitution, which
has been interpreted to reserve the power to
operate schools to the states. Each state’s
system is different, depending on its economic
health, tax system and desires for education.
Historically, Oregon has funded schools
within the middle ranges of states.1 When
the economy is going well, it has funded
above the national average, as in the late
1980s and ‘90s in the graph below. But it
has also dropped significantly below it during
harder times, as in the early part of the new

century. This drop reached its nadir in 2013.
Since then, Oregon spending per student
has almost reached the U.S. average.
There are many reasons for this gain. The
big three are probably a relatively healthy
economy, new taxes for education, and
a COVID-inspired drop in enrollment and
population. Oregon Department of Education
enrollment reports showed a 5.2% drop
from 2019-20 to 2022-23. The comparable
US drop was 2.4% as reported by the
National Center for Education Statistics.
The stimulus funds and other temporary
measures related to the pandemic
make sorting out contributing factors
difficult and deserve further study.

Scope
This report attempts, from a big-picture
point of view, to describe the public sources
Oregon uses to fund early childhood and
K-12 education operations, how those
resources are distributed through the
system and why it was set up that way.
The current expenditure data in the graph
on the previous page is commonly used
to describe and compare school funding
levels. This report uses different data,
but the scope of the analysis is similar:
• The report includes only operational
funding. Funding for capital improvements
may be suitable for a different report.
• Operational expenditures include
instructional, support and
administrative expenditures.
• There is no single source of data that
encompasses the entire fundraising
and distribution system. Because most
funds, including most federal funds, are

distributed or redistributed by the state,
most of the data comes from state agency
budgets as they existed at end of the
2024 legislative session. As a result:
• Because the state budgets in two-year
periods, all figures, unless otherwise
stated, are for two years (the 2023-25
biennium). Thus, they are double the
comparable current expenditure number.
• The scope of this report is slightly larger
than the comparable current expenditure
number because it includes revenues
used for state agency administrative
expenditures and early childhood
expenditures not incurred by public schools
• The figures represent amounts budgeted,
not amounts spent. These differences are
largely timing differences. Funds distributed
to schools will eventually be spent.
For more on how the numbers were
generated, see Appendix A.

Local Property Taxes
Local property taxes were the first
sources of Oregon school funding and,
for many years, by far the largest.
In 1990, the voters passed Measure 5, a
constitutional amendment that phased in
a limit (0.5% of market value) on the total
school operating tax on any property
and explicitly shifted the funding burden
to the state. By 1997, the property tax
share of school funding dropped to 30%,
compared to about 70% in 1990.
As Measure 5 was phasing in, rapidly rising
property values mitigated some of the
expected revenue loss and spawned passage
of a new constitutional initiative further limiting
the property taxes. After a legislative rewrite
correcting major flaws in language, the voters
approved it again in 1997 as Measure 50.
Measure 50 did not repeal Measure 5. It
created a new limit inside of Measure 5. The
Measure 5 limit is absolute – it does not allow
local voters to approve school operating taxes
outside its limit. Measure 50, however, allows
local voters to approve new operating taxes
for up to five years “in the gap” between the
Measure 50 and Measure 5 limits. These “local
option taxes” are raised and spent locally.

State General Fund
State General Fund revenue comes mostly
(95%) from income and income-related
taxes, including the personal income tax,
corporate income taxes, insurance taxes
and estate taxes. The remainder comes from
tobacco taxes, a portion of the profits from
state liquor stores, and fees and charges.
Oregon imposed its first income tax in 1923.
Voters repealed it the next year, but, after
a series of attempts, a permanent tax was
approved and imposed in 1930. The 1930 tax
replaced all statewide property taxes, included
a two-mil levy for schools. In 1947, flush with
income tax revenue from the war-fueled
economy, voters approved an initiative creating
the Basic School Support Fund, increasing the

6

state share to over 30% of school operating
revenue. This share fluctuated over the years
but by 1990 had dropped to under 30%.
Legislative approval of replacement revenue
after approval of Measure 5 increased
the state share to about 70% by 1997.
Corporate Activity Tax
The 2019 Legislature enacted a tax on
corporate activity dedicated to K-12 and early
childhood education. The tax is imposed on
a business’ total sales of goods and services
within Oregon reduced by 35% of the cost
of goods sold or payroll and a $1 million
exemption. A portion of the revenue replaces
school revenue lost by a contemporaneous
personal income tax cut, and the rest is
placed in a dedicated Student Success Fund.
Federal Revenue
Due to constitutional restrictions, federal
mandates and funding largely derive their
authority from protecting the individual rights of
students to an equal education – nutrition and
education programs for low-income students
and schools and partial support for programs
serving students with disabilities. These funds
are typically routed through state departments
of education and distributed to districts as
specified under federal law. Normally, Oregon
schools receive about 7% of their funding
from federal sources. Federal funding for
COVID has increased this share temporarily.
Generally, 25% of revenue received from
U.S. Forest Service land is distributed to the
local County School Fund in the area and
redistributed to the school districts in the area.
Oregon schools also get some federal
revenue that doesn’t go through the state
through competitive grants or payments in
lieu of property taxes on federal facilities.
State Lottery
Voters approved a constitutional initiative
creating the Oregon Lottery in 1985, with

the proceeds dedicated to economic
development. In 1995, voters approved an
amendment allowing lottery revenue to be
used for public education and creating the
Education Stability Fund. In 2016, voters
dedicated a portion of lottery revenue to
fund Outdoor School. Today, roughly 53%
of lottery dollars go to public schools.
Common School Fund
Upon statehood (1859), the federal
government granted 3.4 million acres of
federal lands to the state to support schools.
Under the Oregon Constitution, proceeds from
these lands are deposited into the Common
School Fund, and the fund earnings are
distributed to schools. Many of these lands
were sold over the years. Nonetheless, the
fund balance is currently about $2.1 billion.
The fund is invested in a broad mix of assets.
Up to 3.5% is distributed to schools each year.
State Managed Timber
The Oregon Department of Forestry manages
over 700,000 acres of forest land for the
benefit of local governments in the area.
Most of these lands were returned to the
county to avoid paying property taxes on
the bare land after harvesting or forest fires.
Local taxing districts, including local schools,
receive 63.75% of the gross revenue.
Marijuana Tax
In 2014, Oregon voters approved Measure
91, an initiative enacting laws that legalized
marijuana sales, imposed taxes on sales and
dedicated 40% of the net revenue to schools.
District Revenue
Schools get interest earnings and collect
fees and donations locally for such things
as after school programs, student activities,
events, concessions, school lunches,
facility use and many other things.

Distribution of School Revenue
94% of Oregon School Revenue
is State-Allocated
Most federal revenue is distributed to the
state by formulas and, after allowances for
administration and reservations for state
operated services, distributed within the state
by the Department of Education, which must
ensure the funds are allocated and spent in
accordance with the relevant federal law.
State-raised funds are distributed in a similar
manner, in accordance with state law and
the budgets of the Department of Education,
the Department of Early Learning and Care
and other agencies. Most of the revenue
distributed to districts goes through the State
School Fund. Over the last 10 years, increasing
amounts have been distributed through other
funds and formulas included in the grant-inaid budgets of the Department of Education
and Department of Early Learning and Care.
Most local school operating property
taxes, revenue in lieu of taxes and some
state funds (like the Common School
Fund) are reallocated among districts by
the operation of the State School Fund.
Why the State School Fund
Reallocates Revenue
Before 1990, schools were funded primarily
by local property taxes. These funds were

8

raised and spent locally. Local elections on
permanent and temporary taxing authority
were common and frequently cliff-hangers.
School funding levels varied widely across
the state, reflecting local differences
in ability to get levies passed and the
amount and values of taxable property.
This inequality has been challenged in court.
Since statehood, the Oregon Constitution
has required the “establishment of a
uniform, and general system of common
schools.” But the Oregon Supreme
Court found that these differences were
permissible because the state had required
and provided a minimum standard and the
differences were caused by local voters
who chose to fund at a higher standard.
Nonetheless, school equity has always
been an issue. When the state Basic
Support School Fund was established
in 1947, it devoted a small share to
reducing inequities. In 1974, the Oregon
Legislative Committee on Equal Education
Opportunity completed a major study
of methods of equalizing Oregon school
funding. The results ultimately became a
book regularly cited in academic papers
on school funding. The work, and the
simulation models it produced, became
part of the tools legislative staff used to
support ongoing equity discussions until,
in 1990, Measure 5 changed everything.

Local Funding Differences No
Longer Caused by Local Choice
Measure 5 shifted most of the funding burden
to the state. But that’s not all it did. At full
phase-in, virtually every property in the state
was paying 1% of market value for schools.
Any funding differences that remained were
not caused by local voter choice, but by
differences in property values. So, in addition
to increasing state school funding, the 1990
Legislature created the State School Fund
to equalize district funding over Measure 5’s
phase-in period. Although some details of
the SSF formula have changed, the major
elements remain as they were created.
Since 1990, other sources of school
funding and distribution have come on
line. Nonetheless, the State School Fund
still provides about three-quarters of local
school operating funds. The remaining
sections describe the various formulas
distributing revenue to local schools
through the State School Fund, the Student
Success Fund funded from the corporate
activity tax, and grants funded largely by
the State General Fund and federal funds.
The section attempts to give a basic
explanation of how each method works and
some insight into why it is structured that way.
The appendices contain selected materials
containing more detail on each method.

State School Fund
The purpose of the State School Fund is
to fund local districts equitably - based
on how many students they serve, their
characteristics and other factors that
affect the cost of serving them.
The fund distributes all of the revenue listed
in the distribution table below largely as
unrestricted revenue to school districts. The
State School Fund formulas were developed
based on the following principles:
• To achieve equity, the fund should
encompass as many of the operational
funds available for instruction as
possible. At the time it was created, the
State School Fund allocated almost all
the state and local instructional revenue
available to local schools, with the exception
of some state grants for special education.
• Funding should be per student,
adjusted for conditions or
characteristics that create differences
in costs of serving students.

State School Fund Sources

• Avoid creating incentives to
skew services. Creating complex
factors incentivizes districts to
identify conditions with the highest
payment rates. This skews the
services offered and requires more
effort to authenticate the data. Ideally,
this means using data to estimate
costs that districts do not control.
• Districts should be free to spend the
funds as they see fit and should be
held accountable for the results. The
factors are high-level approximations
designed to roughly, but fairly distribute
the available funds. The data supporting
each factor is not strong enough to
define the service plan. If a district can
achieve good results without spending all
the funds allocated for that characteristic,
it should be emulated, not penalized. If
many districts are doing that, perhaps
the factor should be adjusted.

General Purpose Grant
Most of the work of State School Fund is
done by the General Purpose Grant to school
districts. The General Purpose Grant is a
per-student grant in an amount calculated
to distribute the available statewide funds
to districts. The student counts and each
district’s grant per student are adjusted
to account for differences in the need for
higher-cost services or other local conditions
and structures. The 2022-23 average
grant per student is about $10,700.

The student counts are adjusted by adding
(or subtracting) weights. The adjusted
student count is called the “Weighted
Average Daily Membership” (ADMw).

For example, the poverty weight, based on
census and other independent data, adds
one-quarter of an ADMw for each schoolaged child living in a family below the poverty
level within the district or placed in a foster
home or facility for neglected and delinquent
children. When the formula was created, the
poverty factor was an example of an “ideal”
weight. There is a lot of evidence that living in
poverty inhibits educational achievement.
In creating the weights, the
major issues were:
• Students on an individual education plan
(IEP) have additional needs that vary greatly
in cost. However, experience has shown that
a complex weight schedule creates strong
incentives to place students in more highly
weighted categories. So, the weight was
based on averages to recognize differences
in the incidence of IEPs and capped at 11% of
the unweighted count, which at the time was
at a bit above the average incidence. This
share has since risen so that many districts
are at the cap. The department can approve
a waiver to exceed the cap, and in 2005 the
Legislature established the separate high-cost
disability grant. Taken together, this structure
avoids creating incentives to overidentify
most students and focuses the resources
and administrative process to approve costs
on areas where the costs are highest.
• Although attempts were made to craft an
independent measure of transportation
costs, Oregon’s topography and weather are
too varied to incorporate into a per-student
model. Thus, a separate transportation
grant was established at 70% of costs,
with a detailed definition of eligible costs,
including provisions for boarding schools and
amortized capital costs. This funds most of
the cost, but keeps some incentive to control
costs. The 30% proved too burdensome
for the highest-cost districts. In 2003, the
Legislature increased the state share to 90%
for the 10% highest-cost districts and 80%
for the 10% next highest-cost districts.

Teacher Experience Adjustment
Teacher salaries are the largest single cost of
operating schools. The schedules increase
teacher salaries with experience. Districts with
more experienced teachers thus face higher
costs. The formula partially accounts for this
difference by adjusting each district’s per-student
grant – increasing or decreasing it based on
how much the district’s average experience
level exceeds or trails the statewide average.
The adjustment is proportional to the per-student
grant. For 2023-24, it is about $59 per year of
experience. A district whose teachers, on average,
are one year more experienced than the statewide
average would get $10,759 per student. A district
one year below the average would get $10,641.

ESD Equalization Grant
The 19 education service districts (ESDs)
are regional entities that support the
school districts within their boundaries.
They vary in size and services
across the state but typically provide
specialized instructional services (such
as special education) and administrative
services (such as finance and IT).
The ESD equalization grant “piggy-backs”
on the district general purpose grant by
allocating to each ESD the same share of
the total ESD grant as the districts they
serve (their “component districts”) get from
the district general purpose grant, with a
minimum grant for the smallest districts.
Changing a Factor Does Not
Produce (or Eliminate) any Money
If, for example, you increase the cap
on the IEP weight, the total statewide
weighted student count will rise, and the
grant per student will decrease. Districts
significantly above the 11% cap will gain
revenue, but not as much as they might
think because their grant per student
will drop. Other districts, including some
currently over the cap, will lose revenue.
Unfortunately, at the ground level, the
system looks so complicated that it
masks this dynamic. Many of the State
School Fund revenue sources, for
practical or legal reasons, have their own
distribution formulas. School districts
are required to budget and record these
revenues separately. However, when
these revenues come into a district, the
State School Fund recognizes them and
adjusts state payments to all districts to
equalize the revenue. In the end, each
district’s revenue is not determined by
the individual sources but instead by the
total amount available statewide, and the
district’s share is determined by the State
School Fund formulas and their factors.

Student Success Fund
The 2019 Legislature imposed a corporate
activity tax dedicated to education.
The budgeted 2023-25 distribution of
the tax is shown in the table below.
The 2019 bill also reduced personal income
tax rates. So, after setting aside a reserve,
the tax proceeds are first allocated to the
State School Fund in the amount needed
to replace the revenue lost from the
income tax cut. In addition, $40 million is
allocated to the high-cost disability grant.
The remaining proceeds are
allocated to three accounts:
• At least 50% to the Student Investment
Account – formula grants to K-12
districts and schools to help meet
student mental or behavioral health
needs and improve academic outcomes
of students that have historically
experienced academic disparities.

• Up to 30% to the Statewide Education
Initiative Account – targeted K-12 grants to
achieve the goals above and specifically
to improve early childhood literacy,
high school readiness, high school
completion and reduced absenteeism.
• At least 20% to the Early Learning
Account – support for children from
birth to five years old. Except for
children with identified disabilities,
these services are typically provided
by non-profits and other organizations.
The programs are managed by the
Department of Early Learning and Care.
The actual allocations are set by the
Legislature in the budget process. Because
funding and administration of these programs
are often combined with other sources, the
report will discuss the further distribution
of these funds in the next section.

Special Education
The federal Individuals with Disabilities
Education Act (IDEA) entitles every schoolaged child with a disability to a free and
appropriate public education in the least
restrictive environment. Ultimately, the
responsibility to provide it rests on the
local district for each eligible child within its
boundaries, including those in the justice
system, hospitals and other institutional
settings. Most of these costs are funded from
the district’s State School Fund allocation.
IDEA provides some formula grants distributed
based on prior funding, child census and
poverty counts. Like most federal funding for
elementary and secondary education, the
grants are paid to the state. The state retains
a share for administration and other uses,
distributes the funds to districts and manages
reporting and compliance requirements.
Although these funds are a relatively small
share of total special education costs,
districts can be penalized if they fail to
maintain their prior spending level of nonfederal funds. Districts must carefully manage
the character and timing of spending to
avoid inadvertently triggering a penalty.
IDEA funds can also be spent for services
provided by regional programs. ODE
contracts with eight ESDs and one district
to provide specialized services to children
who are deaf/hard of hearing, or experience
vision impairments, orthopedic impairments,
autism or traumatic brain injury. In addition
to running the School for the Deaf, the state
contracts for educational programs for
students in hospitals, long term care facilities,
youth corrections (YCEP), juvenile corrections
(JCEP) and pediatric nursing. These are largely
General Funded – either directly in the ODE
budget or indirectly by a per-student grant
or a carve-out in the State School Fund.
Early Intervention/
Early Childhood Special Education
IDEA also requires local districts to identify

High School Success (Measure 98)

Conclusion
After the passage of Measure 5, the Legislature overhauled school
finance, equalizing funding per student across the state with mostly
unrestricted funding and the belief that local schools should be
responsible for results. Since then, many things have occurred:
• The 2001 and 2008 recessions hit Oregon particularly hard, and
per-student funding dropped below the national average.
• Buoyed by a rising economy and new funding sources, per-student funding has risen
to near the national average, although the rise occurred partially because Oregon’s
student count dropped more than the national average during the pandemic.
• The perception that the system was lacking in certain areas led
legislators and voters to adopt new requirements and dedicate funding
to specific purposes – career and technical education, improved reading
instruction, high school success, meeting the needs of marginalized
students and dealing with mental health and behavioral issues.
• With the new sources, the Legislature required more community
outreach, educational planning and accountability of districts and
devoted resources to supporting districts in those efforts.
• At the same time, the Legislature devoted significant resources to increasing
the quality and mitigating the rising cost of early childhood education.
The current situation is highly uncertain. Federal COVID funding has largely been
spent. Recovering from the pandemic learning losses is still a challenge. Inflation
is driving up costs. It’s too early to tell which of our new strategies are working.
This report is intended to be a first step toward a conversation about what to do
next. An obvious next step is some analysis of how funds are actually being spent
and to what effect. In the end, the larger question is how to create an efficient,
effective, flexible system that is accountable for results and how to integrate
that system into the community at large to meet the needs of all students.
