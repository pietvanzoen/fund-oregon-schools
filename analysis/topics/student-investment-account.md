# The Student Investment Account: what the money funds and what ODE reports it did

Grantees have reported spending 98.1% to 99.4% of each year's Student Investment Account money, mostly on staff in the years with staff figures (2021-22 on).
The Oregon Department of Education's outcome reports cover two years of targets, and they don't show whether the
money changed outcomes (sources and figures below).

Decisions this informs: standing research (what the money buys). Figures copied from sources: `data/sia_spending.csv`.
Our sums: `derived/sia.csv`. Audits and outside reviews are still to come (#57).

## What the sources say

### What it is and what it can pay for

- The Student Investment Account was created by the Student Success Act in 2019. It gives school districts, eligible
  charter schools and youth corrections and juvenile detention education programs non-competitive grants once they
  meet the application requirements. The Oregon Department of Education (ODE) describes it as receiving at least
  50% of the money deposited in the Fund for Student Success [SRC:ode/sia/2026-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.3];
  by statute the 50% is of what's left after the fund's transfer to the State School Fund and a reserve
  [SRC:legislature/ors/ors327.html].
- The grants are paid in addition to the State School Fund. Their purposes in law are to meet students' mental or
  behavioral health needs, and to increase academic achievement, including reducing academic disparities for the
  student groups the law names [SRC:legislature/ors/ors327.html]. The department calls those groups "focal student
  groups": students of color, students with disabilities, emerging bilingual students, and students navigating
  poverty, homelessness and foster care, among others [SRC:ode/sia/2026-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.3].
- The money may be used only for four kinds of things, each with a list of examples in law: more instructional time
  (for example longer days or years, summer and before- or after-school programs); students' health or safety (for
  example social-emotional learning, mental and behavioral health, school health professionals, trauma-informed
  practices, facility improvements directly related to health or safety); smaller classes or caseloads; and
  well-rounded learning (for example early literacy, middle school supports, art, music, physical education,
  science, technology, engineering and math, career and technical education, electives, college credit, dropout
  prevention, talented and gifted programs, library media educators) [SRC:legislature/ors/ors327.html].
- Each grant is a recipient's share of the money available, by its weighted enrollment. That count gives each
  student in poverty an extra weight of 0.5, where the State School Fund formula uses 0.25, and leaves out virtual
  charter schools [SRC:legislature/ors/ors327.html].

### How grantees report and are held to it

- Each grant recipient must run a yearly financial audit of the grant money, post it and present it at a public
  meeting, and send it to the department. The department then decides each year whether the money was used as the
  grant agreement says; if not, it must work with the recipient on interventions, give technical assistance, direct
  future grant amounts to specific investments, or deduct amounts from future grants (which the recipient may
  appeal to the State Board). It may also deduct money a recipient doesn't commit to spending, and require targeted
  audits [SRC:legislature/ors/ors327.html].
- Grantees with at least 80 students must set Longitudinal Performance Growth Targets for each of five common
  metrics that has at least 10 students:
  four-year graduation, five-year completion, 9th grade on-track, regular attendance and 3rd grade English language
  arts proficiency. The targets are co-developed with the department. "Baseline" and "stretch" targets cover all
  students; "gap-closing" targets cover a combined group of focal students
  [SRC:ode/sia/2026-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.26] [SRC:ode/sia/2026-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.27]. The
  first set was agreed in 2023 for 2023-24 through 2027-28 and written into grant agreements approved by each
  grantee's board [SRC:ode/sia/2026-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.27]
  [SRC:ode/sia/2026-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.39].
- In 2025 the department paused adding more years of targets in anticipation of changes from the Education
  Accountability Act, Senate Bill 141, passed in 2025 [SRC:ode/sia/2026-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.27]
  [SRC:ode/sia/2026-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.3]. The department's January 2026 report said grantees would
  co-develop new Performance Growth Targets with it in spring 2026, adding K-2 regular attenders and 8th grade math
  [SRC:ode/sia/2026-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.27].
- Separately, the department's 2026 guidance on local Performance Growth Targets under SB 141, which doesn't mention
  the Student Investment Account, set Aug. 31, 2026 for all targets to be submitted, covering the four years
  beginning in 2026-27, and Sept. 15, 2026 for the department to review and approve them; once approved, they can't
  be changed [SRC:ode/accountability/pgt-co-development-guidance.pdf p.3]
  [SRC:ode/accountability/pgt-co-development-guidance.pdf p.4]. As of Nov. 22, 2026, the archive has no
  record of whether the department approved the local targets by Sept. 15.
- Grantees also rate themselves on 15 "progress markers" (a 0-4 scale) and file a yearly narrative report
  [SRC:ode/sia/2025-09_progress-markers-annual-reporting-2023-24.pdf p.4]. The department groups the markers by when
  it expects them: early signs (three to six months), intermediate changes (six to 18 months) and substantial
  changes (18 months or more) [SRC:ode/sia/2025-09_progress-markers-annual-reporting-2023-24.pdf p.6]. In 2023-24
  the ratings were quarterly; for 2025-27 they are to cover quarters 2 and 4
  [SRC:ode/sia/2025-09_progress-markers-annual-reporting-2023-24.pdf p.5]. Grantees are not required or expected to
  meet all progress markers; the department uses them to target technical assistance and coaching
  [SRC:ode/sia/2025-09_progress-markers-annual-reporting-2023-24.pdf p.5].
- As of the department's January 2026 report, the account is part of its Integrated Programs, which combine
  several grant programs in one application. Grantees first applied through Integrated Programs in spring 2023,
  and again in spring 2025 for 2025-29 [SRC:ode/sia/2026-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.3]
  [SRC:ode/sia/2025-09_progress-markers-annual-reporting-2023-24.pdf p.4].

### How much was spent, by year

- Grantees reported spending 98.1% to 99.4% of each year's money (of the allocation, or for 2024-25 of the amount
  claimed), from $147,632,073 in 2020-21 to $551,495,257
  in 2024-25 [SRC:ode/sia/2022-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.13] [SRC:ode/sia/2023-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.16]
  [SRC:ode/sia/2024-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.22] [SRC:ode/sia/2025-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.19]
  [SRC:ode/sia/2026-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.23]. The department reports spending in six categories: the law's four uses plus ongoing
  community engagement and administration (allowed indirect costs and grant administration)
  [SRC:ode/sia/2026-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.23]. The figures are copied into `data/sia_spending.csv`; the
  2021-22 figures were extracted when 249 of 262 grantees had reported
  [SRC:ode/sia/2023-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.16].

- About $472 million had been projected for the first year; the Legislature reduced it to $150 million in an August
  2020 special session because of COVID-19's effect on the Corporate Activity Tax
  [SRC:ode/sia/2022-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.13].
- **Health and safety has been the largest category every year:** 41.7% of the allocation in 2020-21, and of
  spending 33.9% in 2021-22, 36.7% in 2022-23, 35.7% in 2023-24 and 33.5% ($184.9 million) in 2024-25.
  Well-rounded education and smaller classes come next [SRC:ode/sia/2022-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.13]
  [SRC:ode/sia/2023-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.17] [SRC:ode/sia/2024-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.23]
  [SRC:ode/sia/2025-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.19] [SRC:ode/sia/2026-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.23].
- **Most of it pays for people.** Salaries and benefits were 75.7% of spending in 2021-22, 81.7% in 2022-23 and in
  2023-24, and 85.8% ($473.3 million) in 2024-25 [SRC:ode/sia/2023-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.17]
  [SRC:ode/sia/2024-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.23] [SRC:ode/sia/2025-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.19]
  [SRC:ode/sia/2026-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.24]. In 2021-22, salary and benefit spending funded 2,459.0
  full-time-equivalent licensed positions; the largest groups were general education teachers (624.5), mental
  health professionals, psychologists and licensed clinical social workers (273.4) and school counselors (202.1).
  Those two counselor groups were 19.3% of the licensed positions
  [SRC:ode/sia/2023-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.19]. In 2024-25, $282.7 million went to licensed salaries; the
  department notes its benefits figure is an underestimate, because some benefits were reported as salaries
  [SRC:ode/sia/2026-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.24].
- The 2024-25 report prints the total spent as $551,495,257 in two places and as $551,495,297 once
  [SRC:ode/sia/2026-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.23].

### What ODE reports about results

- The department describes the share of grantees meeting their targets as mixed by metric and student group
  [SRC:ode/sia/2026-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.41].
- **2024-25 targets (second year).** Of grantees required to set each target, these met their baseline (all
  students) target: 9th grade on-track 124 of 167 (74.3%); regular attendance 93 of 187 (49.7%); 3rd grade English
  language arts 66 of 171 (38.6%) [SRC:ode/sia/2026-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.39]
  [SRC:ode/sia/2026-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.40].
- For gap-closing targets: 9th grade on-track 85 of 137 (62.0%), regular attendance 122 of 182 (67.0%), 3rd grade
  English language arts 55 of 131 (42.0%) [SRC:ode/sia/2026-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.39]
  [SRC:ode/sia/2026-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.40].
- The department reports the median grantee's change from 2023-24 to 2024-25, for all students, as 2.21% in 9th
  grade on-track, 1.78% in 3rd grade English language arts and 0.01% in regular attendance
  [SRC:ode/sia/2026-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.39] [SRC:ode/sia/2026-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.40].
  Neither this report nor the department's 2023-24 report on targets and results, which prints median changes the
  same way, says whether such changes are percentage points
  [SRC:ode/sia/2024-12_lpgts-and-actuals-2023-24.pdf p.1].
- For 2023-24, more than half of grantees met their four-year graduation baseline and gap-closing targets; half met
  their five-year completion baseline target and 46.5% the gap-closing one; graduation and completion data for
  2024-25 come out later [SRC:ode/sia/2026-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.28]
  [SRC:ode/sia/2026-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.39].
- The department calls the 9th grade on-track change "substantial" and says the larger median gains for focal
  students in attendance and on-track indicate "progress is being made to close the gaps"
  [SRC:ode/sia/2026-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.41].
- The department says targets vary a great deal among grantees, and that small grantees can see large jumps or
  drops when one student changes a metric by several points [SRC:ode/sia/2026-01_sia-legislative-report.pdf p.27].
- From grantees' 2023-24 self-ratings, the department found most had made "lower order systemic changes" and were
  making steady progress toward higher-order changes. In its summary of the ratings and narrative reports
  together, it names academics (including career connected learning), data systems and community engagement as
  areas of great success; hiring, especially of mental and behavioral health professionals in rural areas, and 9th
  grade on-track and course scheduling are areas of ongoing work
  [SRC:ode/sia/2025-09_progress-markers-annual-reporting-2023-24.pdf p.4].

## Analysis

The spending picture is clear and consistent: grantees (districts, eligible charter schools, and youth corrections
and juvenile detention programs) reported spending almost all of each year's allocation, mostly on staff (from 2021-22, when staff figures begin), in the
same three categories every year. Health and safety leads every year. By the law's list, that category covers
student mental and behavioral health and school health professionals, among other health and safety uses, which
fits the first of the law's two purposes. The reports don't break the category down further, but in 2021-22, 19.3%
of the licensed positions the account funded were mental health professionals or school counselors (source above).

The results picture is much weaker, for three reasons. First, the department compares each grantee with targets
the grantee set with the department, not with what would have happened without the money, so a met target doesn't
show the account caused the change; that's our reasoning, not the department's. Second, from the department's
reports: there are only two years of target data (2023-24 and 2024-25), and the department paused adding years to
the targets in 2025 in anticipation of SB 141, planning new ones for spring 2026. The statewide SB 141 targets are
in `topics/results.md`. Third, also from its reports: the progress markers are self-ratings that grantees are not
expected to meet in full. On the department's figures, about three in four grantees met their baseline 9th grade
on-track target in 2024-25, about half met their attendance target, and fewer than two in five met their 3rd grade
English language arts target. Whether that is good depends on how ambitious the targets were, which the department
says varied a great deal. The department's median changes (2.21%, 1.78%, 0.01%) are most likely percentage points:
its overview of how the targets were set uses "%" for a percentage-point difference ("nearly two percentage points
higher (1.9%)") [SRC:ode/sia/2023-2028_lpgt-overview-of-targets.pdf p.2]. That's our reading; the department has
been asked (#116).

So the archive can say what the account's money paid for, and how grantees did against their own targets in two
recent years. It can't yet say whether the account changed outcomes statewide. The Quality Education Commission
recommends sustaining Student Success Act investments (`topics/qem-practices-outcomes.md`). Outside audits and
reviews (#57), outcomes over time (#58) and the research on whether funding changes outcomes (#63) bear on that.
