# The Quality Education Model: the state's estimate of what schools need

Decisions this informs: `decisions.md#ssf-2027-29`, `decisions.md#cost-model-2027`. Figures copied from sources:
`data/qem_gap.csv`, `data/ssf_history.csv`.

## Analysis

The 2026 Quality Education Model report gives three 2027-29 figures, and they answer different questions: the State
School Fund's current service level ($12.56 billion, the amount needed to continue existing programs, a Department
of Administrative Services estimate) [SRC:ode/qec/2026_qem_report.pdf p.103], the status quo ($13.78 billion of state funding to keep 2025-27 service levels) and the full model
($16.21 billion to meet the state's goals) [SRC:ode/qec/2026_qem_report.pdf p.117]
[SRC:gov/press/2024_ssf-csl-proposal.html]. The current service level is the State School Fund alone; the
status quo and the full model count other state money too. So the full model's $3.65 billion above the current service
level compares a fund with a broader total, and its $2.42 billion above the status quo is the like-for-like gap between
the model's two scenarios (sources below).

**What a State School Fund at the current service level would mean.** The model's status quo is about $1.2 billion
more than the current service level. That $1.2 billion is not part of the current service level. It is the share of
the status quo the model leaves to state money outside the State School Fund. If that money isn't there, the report
says, it is left to reduced services. The table that works out what the state must pay lists no state grant program among the revenue it
subtracts (below). The state does pay for grant programs outside the fund. The Legislature's Joint Committee on Public
Education Appropriation counted $2.2 billion of them as core K-12 funding in 2025-27, more than the model's $1.2
billion for 2027-29. Slides in the commission's October 2026 meeting materials cite the same figure. By our sum, $1.65
billion of those grants are paid from the Corporate Activity Tax, a state business tax [DER:csl/core_grants_cat]. For 2027-29, the December 2026 revenue
forecast (dated Nov. 18, 2026) gives the tax money for two of its grant accounts, the Student Investment Account
and the Statewide Education Initiatives Account. Our sum of the two is $2.00 billion [DER:csl/sia_seia_2027_29],
$0.78 billion more than the model's $1.2 billion [DER:csl/sia_seia_minus_non_ssf]. That is a revenue forecast, not a
budget, and the second account also pays for statewide initiatives and educator training, not only grants to
districts.

Whether those grants cover the $1.2 billion depends on a point the archive can't settle yet: whether the status quo
prices what the grants pay for and doesn't already subtract their money. A question to Department of Education staff
is drafted (#146). The sources point both ways (below). The report says the status quo covers all current activities
"regardless of the historical funding source", and the Legislature's committee says the 2024 model's revenue
calculations didn't count state grants. The model's cost workbook subtracts only local, federal and food service revenue, plus a PERS side account line, with
no line for state grant revenue. But the
report's appendix says revenue in the Student Investment Account and High School Success funds is "netted out" within
its local, federal and food service revenue lines, without saying how much. The commission's October 2026 slides also
list fitting the grants into the model as a challenge. If the status quo does price what the grants pay for, a State
School Fund at the current service level keeps the model's statewide status quo as long as the grant programs
continue at about their 2025-27 amounts. If it doesn't, the grants can't be set against the $1.2 billion, and the
report's own wording applies: that share is left to other state money or to reduced services. The difference between
the two readings is large, roughly the size of the grants themselves. Grants are also tied to specific purposes, so
even in total they may not be able to pay for every cost in the status quo, such as replacing lost federal money.
Neither reading reaches the full model's $16.21 billion, and the commission's own view is that the current service level
"reflects and perpetuates historical budgetary choices" (below).

The model's status quo is a statewide measure, not a forecast of any district's budget. The current service level is
a mechanical update of the last budget for pay, benefits, pension rates, inflation and enrollment. It leaves out
declining federal money and falling earnings on the pension side accounts districts hold with the Public Employees
Retirement System (PERS), accounts whose earnings offset part of their pension costs; the model's status quo includes
both (below). So a district can have less to spend than its current services cost in a year when statewide funding
matches the model's status quo, for example where federal pandemic money paid for services. The archive has no
district-level estimate of what a budget at the current service level would mean for services, and the commission
says it can't predict where reductions from lost federal money would land. Whether a budget at the current service
level keeps today's services therefore depends on the yardstick. By the model's statewide status quo, it does if the
status quo prices what the grants pay for and the grants continue, and it may not otherwise. By a given district's costs,
it may not.

Funding for 2025-27 that the Senate President's and House Speaker's offices called "Record Funding"
[SRC:legislature/press/2025-05-28_record-k12-funding.pdf p.1] and a gap in 2027-29 can both be true: they measure
different two-year budgets against different estimates. Our reading is that the news figures of "$2.5B more" and "$5B more"
compare the model with the 2025-27 budget, not with the current service level, so they aren't comparable with the
figures here and below.

The Quality Education Model is the state's own estimate, but it is contested: the Legislature's Joint Committee on Public
Education Appropriation found 2025-27 funding sufficient by it while criticizing its method, and the 2027 Legislature may replace it. A gap measured against the model is a
gap against one benchmark.

**If the model is changed or replaced.** Senate Bill 1555 (2026), the joint committee's bill to move the cost model to
the Legislature's research office, did not pass, so the Quality Education Commission and the model remain
in law, and the 2026 model is the commission's estimate on record for 2027-29. Of the comparison figures on this page, the
full model and the status quo are the model's scenarios; they would lose their standing as the state's benchmark if
the model were replaced. The current service level, the adopted 2025-27
amount and, once released, the governor's 2027-29 budget do not depend on it.

If the 2027 Legislature passed a bill shaped like SB 1555 as introduced, the yardstick for 2027-29 would not be the
2026 model. The report published after Nov. 1, 2027, would follow the 2027 session, so it would be the one on 2027-29
funding. For it, the Department of Administrative Services would adjust the costs in the joint committee's Nov. 19, 2025, report (the one that found
2025-27 funding closes the gap) for inflation and other costs. A new model would come later: the 2026 bill's dates
put the first one in 2028, for the report after Nov. 1, 2029. A 2027 bill could set other dates.

The goals would change too: from today's goals to every district counting as a "standard" district (which SB 1555 would have
redefined as one that complies with state and federal law), licensed and qualified teachers, and the statewide targets for the metrics
in Senate Bill 141 (2025) (sources below). A dollar figure from a new model would then price a different target than the current model's and
would not be directly comparable with it. Then "the gap" would depend on which estimate a reader uses, and on who built it: a
governor-appointed commission (today) or a contractor hired by the Legislature's research office, with a
legislative committee judging sufficiency in both cases.

Per-student spending: a claim that Oregon spends above the national average per student is not yet sourced here. The
model report's ranking is of state-sourced funding, which is a different measure from total spending.

## What the sources say

### The 2027-29 figures

- The 2026 Quality Education Model report puts the State School Fund's current service level at $12,558,429,348, the
  Department of Administrative Services' estimate including the Student Success Act transfer
  [SRC:ode/qec/2026_qem_report.pdf p.103]. The current service level is the amount needed in the next two-year budget
  to continue existing authorized programs [SRC:gov/press/2024_ssf-csl-proposal.html].
- The report puts the core state funding needed to keep 2025-27 service levels (the "status quo") at $13.78 billion,
  and the core state funding needed to meet the state's goals (the "full QEM") at $16.21 billion
  [SRC:ode/qec/2026_qem_report.pdf p.117].
- The full model is $2.42 billion more than the status quo and $3.65 billion more than "the SSF CSL estimate
  available at the time of this report's publication" [SRC:ode/qec/2026_qem_report.pdf p.117].
- The status quo counts state funding beyond the State School Fund: $1,217,461,216 of it is state money outside the
  fund [SRC:ode/qec/2026_qem_report.pdf p.103].
- The Quality Education Commission leaves "the determination of adequacy and which state funding streams and grant
  programs should count toward that to the Legislature" [SRC:ode/qec/2026_qem_report.pdf p.117].
- The Legislature adopted $11.36 billion for the State School Fund for 2025-27
  [SRC:fiscal/lfo/2025-07_lab-summary-2025-27.pdf p.1], described in a press release from the offices of the Senate
  President and the House Speaker as $11.4 billion [SRC:legislature/press/2025-05-28_record-k12-funding.pdf p.1].
  Gov. Tina Kotek had proposed $11.36 billion [NEWS:NEWS-20241203-osba-1].
- The Portland Public Schools board cites a $12.57 billion current service level [PPSARCH:BB:14673756 p.1].
- News coverage reported the model's estimate of need as "$2.5B more" and "$5B more" [NEWS:NEWS-20260812-opb-1].
- By 2024 Census data, Oregon ranked 20th in state-sourced per-pupil funding; funding at the model's level would move it
  to 12th [SRC:ode/qec/2026_qem_report.pdf p.117].

### The status quo's $1.2 billion of state money outside the State School Fund

- The report's 2027-29 status quo is $24,754,756,554 of total costs for districts and education service districts,
  less $10,978,865,990 of net non-state revenue (local revenue in and outside the formula, federal revenue and food
  service revenue, with a $245,220,477 line for Public Employees Retirement System (PERS) side account earnings net of
  debt service obligations). That leaves $13,775,890,564 for the state [SRC:ode/qec/2026_qem_report.pdf p.102]
  [SRC:ode/qec/2026_qem_report.pdf p.103]. Taking away the State School Fund's current service level of $12,558,429,348 leaves
  $1,217,461,216 of "Remaining Non-SSF State Funds for Status Quo Core K-12 Education Funding"
  [SRC:ode/qec/2026_qem_report.pdf p.103]. The revenues subtracted in that table are all non-state; no state grant
  program is listed [SRC:ode/qec/2026_qem_report.pdf p.103].
- The report doesn't say which programs make up the remainder. Its summary says 4.9% of the estimated need is left to
  be covered by state money from outside the State School Fund or by reducing services
  [SRC:ode/qec/2026_qem_report.pdf p.13].
- The status quo prices all current K-12 activities at the prior budget's level "regardless of the historical funding
  source of those activities", net of non-state revenue. If federal or local funding falls, it includes the state cost
  of replacing it [SRC:ode/qec/2026_qem_report.pdf p.101]. It uses the actual base-year staffing ratios for schools
  of about the prototype schools' size [SRC:ode/qec/2026_qem_report.pdf p.93].
- The report says the current service level covers the State School Fund only. It excludes activities paid for from
  outside the fund, "such as grants and funds for the Student Success Act other than the SSA transfer to the SSF";
  changes in non-state funding, such as declining federal funds; and cost increases outside the fund, such as
  declining PERS side account earnings [SRC:ode/qec/2026_qem_report.pdf p.100].
- The report's appendix says revenue from the Student Investment Account (Fund 251) and Measure 98 High School
  Success (Fund 252), among other funds outside the State School Fund, is "netted out of the total funding amount
  required" in its local revenue outside the formula, federal revenue, food service and PERS side account lines, "in
  order to include only funds that are distributed through the State School Fund (SSF) in the model". It lists the
  accounting codes used for each line [SRC:ode/qec/2026_qem_report.pdf p.129].
- The model's cost workbook (Appendix C) has a revenue sheet that lists local revenue outside and in the formula, food
  service enterprise revenue and federal revenue (the federal lines by source code, 4100 to 4900); it lists no state
  grant revenue. Its output table subtracts these, with the local line labelled "Less: Local Revenue not in Formula
  (local option taxes, fees, grants, donations, etc.)", and adjusts for PERS side account earnings
  [SRC:ode/qec/2026_qem_appendix-c_cost-model.xlsx].
- The Corporate Activity Tax pays into both the fund and the grants. Part of it goes into the State School Fund, and
  additional money from it is distributed to school districts as grants for specific purposes, the Legislative Policy
  and Research Office explains
  [SRC:legislature/lpro/2025-08_k12-education-funding-background.pdf p.4].
- The Statewide Education Initiatives Account, one of the three accounts the Fund for Student Success
  splits its remaining revenue into after its transfers to the State School Fund,
  supports "investments in statewide initiatives and educator professional development", for example the High School
  Success, Hunger Free Schools, Early Literacy Success and recovery schools programs
  [SRC:legislature/olis/2025I1/310959_jpea-adequacy-report.pdf p.15] [SRC:legislature/olis/2025I1/310959_jpea-adequacy-report.pdf p.16].
- The Joint Committee on Public Education Appropriation's report adopted Nov. 19, 2025, says the 2024 model's revenue
  calculations "do not account for state grant-in-aid received by school districts, including the grants funded
  through the Fund for Student Success" [SRC:legislature/olis/2025I1/310959_jpea-adequacy-report.pdf p.24].
- That report lists $2,202,344,784 of state grants as core K-12 funding for 2025-27: Student Investment Account grants
  ($1,109,828,482), specialized student service grants ($445,987,875), High School Success grants ($338,078,919),
  Hunger Free Schools ($127,345,728), Early Literacy School grants ($93,780,000), Summer Learning grants ($70,000,000)
  and the Oregon School for the Deaf ($17,323,780). The table splits each line between General Fund and Fund for
  Student Success (Corporate Activity Tax) money. It lists another $1,378,102,193 as "Additional state funding to
  strengthen the K-12 education system", most of it early learning programs ($1,115,618,619)
  [SRC:legislature/olis/2025I1/310959_jpea-adequacy-report.pdf p.29].
- Slides in the materials for the commission's Oct. 21, 2026, meeting cite the same $2.2 billion of state K-12
  support outside the fund and say "Adequacy evaluation can no longer look at the State School Fund in isolation; it
  must account for total state K–12 investments" [SRC:ode/qec/minutes/2026-10-21.pdf p.1]
  [SRC:ode/qec/minutes/2026-10-21.pdf p.63]. The same slide lists an "Integration Challenge: Incorporating targeted
  categorical grants into a model historically structured around formula-driven core prototype resources"
  [SRC:ode/qec/minutes/2026-10-21.pdf p.63].
- The December 2026 forecast, dated Nov. 18, 2026 [SRC:fiscal/oea/2026-12_forecast.pdf p.1], puts Corporate Activity
  Tax money for 2027-29 at $3.41 billion [SRC:fiscal/oea/2026-12_forecast.pdf p.34]: $910.903 million to the State School Fund, $1,250.421 million to the
  Student Investment Account, $750.253 million to the Statewide Education Initiatives Account and $500.168 million to
  the Early Learning Account [SRC:fiscal/oea/2026-12_forecast.pdf p.75].

### How the current service level is calculated

- The Department of Administrative Services' 2027-29 budget instructions say the current service level "estimates the
  cost to continue legislatively approved programs into the 2027-29 biennium" and is required by law
  [SRC:fiscal/das/2026-03_budget-instructions-2027-29.pdf p.30]. It starts from the 2025-27 approved budget, adjusted
  for the 2026 session, and adds "essential packages" such as inflation and phase-ins of approved program changes
  [SRC:fiscal/das/2026-03_budget-instructions-2027-29.pdf p.6]. The standard inflation factors state agencies use for the two years of 2027-29
  are 4.9% for general inflation, 9.3% for non-state employee personnel costs and 6.5% for medical services
  [SRC:fiscal/das/2026-03_budget-instructions-2027-29.pdf p.6].
- The executive branch calculates it. The Joint Committee on Ways and Means considers it, but "the calculated CSL
  amount does not limit the committee's ability to set any amount it chooses". House Bill 2140 (2025) put the 49/51
  split of the fund between the two school years into law, and future current service levels are based on the
  second, larger year [SRC:legislature/lpro/2025-08_k12-education-funding-background.pdf p.5].
- For the State School Fund, the commission's report describes these steps: start from the prior approved budget;
  remove one-time money; phase programs in or out; take out the set-asides the law carves out of the fund; and use the
  Legislative Revenue Office's estimate of local revenue. Personnel, 85% of the formula base, is projected with the
  Department of Education's educator compensation model, with health benefits assumed to grow at the 3.4% annual
  cap set by Senate Bill 1067 (2017) even when premiums rise less. The other 15%, services and supplies, gets the Department of Administrative Services' standard inflation rate. Then
  the total is adjusted for projected enrollment [SRC:ode/qec/2026_qem_report.pdf p.111].
- Enrollment in that step is unweighted resident enrollment, which the report puts at 537,748 students in 2024-25 and
  530,225 in 2026-27. Districts are paid on weighted enrollment, the higher of the current or prior year, which
  cushions a declining district's share but not the statewide total [SRC:ode/qec/2026_qem_report.pdf p.114].
- Three changes Gov. Tina Kotek directed in 2024 (the 49/51 split, a 10-year rather than 20-year window for
  compensation, and yearly local property tax estimates) added an estimated $515 million to the 2025-27 current
  service level and carry forward into later budgets [SRC:ode/qec/2026_qem_report.pdf p.114]
  [SRC:gov/press/2024_ssf-csl-proposal.html].
- **PERS.** The 2025-27 current service level assumed employer contributions to PERS of 20.45% in both years
  [SRC:legislature/olis/2025R1/293759_ssf-budget-review.pdf p.3], and includes standard adjustments for changes in
  PERS rates [SRC:legislature/olis/2025R1/307212_sb5516-ssf-work-session.pdf p.2]. The commission calls the way the
  current service level handles PERS "a primary area of divergence". Its table projects the blended PERS rate rising from 15.16% in 2024-25 to 24.38% in 2027-28, as side account earnings fall from 11.87% to
  1.85% [SRC:ode/qec/2026_qem_report.pdf p.113]. Districts' pension bond debt service is projected to peak at over $465.6 million in
  2026-27 [SRC:ode/qec/2026_qem_report.pdf p.114].
- The commission's view is that the current service level "reflects and perpetuates historical budgetary choices":
  a budget below need lowers the base for every later calculation [SRC:ode/qec/2026_qem_report.pdf p.104].

### What districts would keep or lose

- The commission says that if lost federal money isn't replaced with state money, districts' ability to keep
  services falls, and that without knowing the cuts districts choose, "it is impossible to predict the exact locus
  or magnitude of these declines" [SRC:ode/qec/2026_qem_report.pdf p.101]. It says districts will need to make cuts
  if alternative funding isn't found to at least partly offset declining federal resources
  [SRC:ode/qec/2026_qem_report.pdf p.102].
- It says that when state allocations fall short of contract costs, school boards respond mid-budget with larger
  classes, cut programs, shorter school years or layoffs [SRC:ode/qec/2026_qem_report.pdf p.113].

### Whether the Quality Education Model is the right yardstick

- The Joint Committee on Public Education Appropriation's November 2025 report says 2025-27 funding "effectively closes the
  funding gap and allows for the full implementation of the QEM" [SRC:legislature/olis/2025I1/310695_jpea.pdf p.4].
- The same report criticizes how the model is built [SRC:legislature/olis/2025I1/310695_jpea.pdf p.5].
- A legislative concept from the joint committee would move the cost model from the Quality Education Commission to the Legislative
  Policy and Research Office [SRC:legislature/olis/2025I1/311607_jpea-legislative-concepts.pdf p.1]. It became
  Senate Bill 1555 (2026), described by Rep. Ricki Ruiz, D-District 50 (Gresham), as replacing the model
  [NEWS:NEWS-20260121-katu-1] [SRC:legislature/members/ruiz.html].
- The overhaul effort led by Ruiz and Sen. Janeen Sollman, D-District 15 (Forest Grove, Cornelius, Hillsboro, Rock
  Creek), was unsuccessful, and Sen. Lew Frederick, D-District 22 (N/NE Portland), said he expects significant changes
  in 2027 [NEWS:NEWS-20260617-occ-1] [SRC:legislature/members/sollman.html] [SRC:legislature/members/frederick.html].
- In November 2026, Ruiz described a long-term vision of a new funding system with an adequate resource needs cost model
  that increases the number of prototype school models [SRC:legislature/social/2026-11-19_ruiz-instagram-osba-panel.txt].

### Senate Bill 1555 (2026): what the joint committee proposed in the model's place

- **Status.** Senate Bill 1555 did not pass. It was still in the Senate Committee on Education when the 2026
  session's final business concluded March 6 [SRC:ode/legislation/2026_summary-enacted-education-legislation.pdf p.4]
  [SRC:legislature/bills/2026R1_SB1555_overview.html], and the commission's October 2026 meeting materials list
  "2026 Senate Bill 1555 (Did not pass)" [SRC:ode/qec/minutes/2026-10-21.pdf p.78]. The bullets below say what it
  would have done as introduced.
- **Who does the work today.** The Quality Education Commission is appointed by the governor and decides what goes
  into the model; the Oregon Department of Education runs it; in odd-numbered years the joint committee considers the
  model's report and issues its own sufficiency determination [SRC:legislature/olis/2026R1/313103_sb1555-genesis-memo.pdf p.1].
- **Where the bill came from.** In 2024 some commissioners asked for money to "modernize" the model; Senate Bill 1552
  (2024) required an outside evaluation first, and the American Institutes for Research found that in many ways the
  model was not aligned with professional best practices [SRC:legislature/olis/2026R1/313103_sb1555-genesis-memo.pdf p.1].
  The joint committee introduced SB 1555 to carry out the evaluation's recommendations and the nine opportunities for
  improvement in its November 2025 report [SRC:legislature/olis/2026R1/313103_sb1555-genesis-memo.pdf p.2]. It was
  the joint committee's legislative concept LC 43, in its Jan. 15, 2026, memo of legislative concepts for
  introduction [SRC:legislature/olis/2025I1/311607_jpea-legislative-concepts.pdf p.1], and was introduced at the
  committee's request [SRC:legislature/bills/2026R1_SB1555_introduced.pdf p.1].
- **New quality goals.** The goals the cost model would have priced were: every district meeting the requirements
  to count as a "standard" school district; every teacher licensed or registered and qualified for the subject or
  grade taught; and meeting the statewide targets for the metrics in section 327.165 of the Oregon Revised Statutes
  [SRC:legislature/bills/2026R1_SB1555_introduced.pdf p.1], the metrics identified in Senate Bill 141 (2025)
  [SRC:legislature/olis/2026R1/313691_sb1555-staff-measure-summary.pdf p.1]. Under current law a district is
  presumed standard until the state finds it deficient; the bill would have redefined it as complying with state and
  federal law [SRC:legislature/bills/2026R1_SB1555_introduced.pdf p.4]. It would also have repealed the existing
  goals language [SRC:legislature/olis/2025I1/311607_jpea-legislative-concepts.pdf p.1].
- **Who would have built the model.** The Legislative Policy and Research Director would have contracted with a
  public or private entity to build a cost model identifying the resources needed to meet those goals and their
  cost, using research, evidence and the professional judgment of currently practicing Oregon educators
  [SRC:legislature/bills/2026R1_SB1555_introduced.pdf p.2]. The prototypes would have been of schools and school
  districts, taking into account differences in size and location, the student groups that get extra weight in the
  formula (section 327.013 (1)(c) of the Oregon Revised Statutes) and fixed costs [SRC:legislature/bills/2026R1_SB1555_introduced.pdf p.2]
  [SRC:legislature/bills/2026R1_SB1555_introduced.pdf p.3] [SRC:legislature/ors/ors327.html]; the model would also have
  prioritized resources in case of limited funding [SRC:legislature/bills/2026R1_SB1555_introduced.pdf p.2]
  [SRC:legislature/bills/2026R1_SB1555_introduced.pdf p.3]. The resource specifications for each prototype would
  have gone to the Department of Education to share with districts and post on its website
  [SRC:legislature/bills/2026R1_SB1555_introduced.pdf p.3].
- **Who would have judged sufficiency.** The Joint Interim Committee on Ways and Means would have prepared the
  sufficiency report, using the cost model, within 180 days after an odd-year session adjourns
  [SRC:legislature/bills/2026R1_SB1555_introduced.pdf p.2]. The bill would have abolished the Joint Committee on Public
  Education Appropriation and the Quality Education Commission [SRC:legislature/bills/2026R1_SB1555_introduced.pdf p.1] by repealing sections 171.857
  and 327.500 of the Oregon Revised Statutes, among others, on Jan. 1, 2027 [SRC:legislature/bills/2026R1_SB1555_introduced.pdf p.19]; those are
  the statutes for the Committee on Public Education Appropriation and the Quality Education Commission
  [SRC:legislature/ors/ors171.html] [SRC:legislature/ors/ors327.html]. Because it did not pass, both remain in law.
- **How often.** A new cost model at least once every eight years; in the two-year budget periods between, the
  Department of Administrative Services would have updated the model's cost estimates for inflation and other costs,
  consistent with how the governor's budget is prepared [SRC:legislature/bills/2026R1_SB1555_introduced.pdf p.3].
  The research office would not have had to contract for a model if the Legislature didn't fund the contract
  [SRC:legislature/bills/2026R1_SB1555_introduced.pdf p.3].
- **Timing.** The first model was to be finished by Feb. 1, 2028, and first used for the report published after
  Nov. 1, 2029; the new sections, including the 180-day deadline [SRC:legislature/bills/2026R1_SB1555_introduced.pdf p.2],
  would have applied only from then. For the report
  published after Nov. 1, 2027 (the next one, after the 2027 session), the Department of Administrative Services would have adjusted the
  costs in the report adopted Nov. 19, 2025, for inflation and other costs
  [SRC:legislature/bills/2026R1_SB1555_introduced.pdf p.3] [SRC:legislature/bills/2026R1_SB1555_introduced.pdf p.4].
  That is the joint committee's adequacy report [SRC:legislature/olis/2025I1/310959_jpea-adequacy-report.pdf p.1].
- **Amendments.** Legislative Policy and Research Office staff summarized three proposed amendments (-1, -2, -5).
  All three would have replaced the standard-school-district goal with complying with applicable state and federal
  laws; the -2 and -5 would have added a public hearing before the model is adopted, professional judgment panels of
  currently practicing Oregon educators, a statewide panel of them to make final decisions (-2) or final
  recommendations (-5) on the resources in the model, and a preliminary report; the -5 would also have changed the
  interval for a new model to six years [SRC:legislature/olis/2026R1/313691_sb1555-staff-measure-summary.pdf p.2].
  The summary says it was not adopted or endorsed by the committee; the amendment texts and any committee votes on
  them aren't archived [SRC:legislature/olis/2026R1/313691_sb1555-staff-measure-summary.pdf p.1].
- **The commission's side.** Responding to the American Institutes for Research evaluation in its latest report, the commission says it is
  exploring more adequacy goals and agrees more prototype schools should be added but has lacked the staff to do so
  [SRC:ode/qec/2026_qem_report.pdf p.99]; see `topics/qem-practices-outcomes.md` for its response in full.
- **Hearings and issues raised.** The Senate Committee on Education met on it Feb. 10 and Feb. 12, 2026; issues
  discussed included the credibility of the current and proposed systems, the interval between cost model studies,
  current sufficiency and district deficits, and the dedication of those who have served as Quality Education
  Commissioners [SRC:legislature/olis/2026R1/313691_sb1555-staff-measure-summary.pdf p.1]. Hearing testimony and
  recordings aren't archived.
- **Where the record shows it stopped.** The Legislature's bill-tracking site, as archived on Nov. 21, 2026,
  lists the bill's current location as the Senate Committee on Education, with no chapter number (an enacted bill
  gets one) [SRC:legislature/bills/2026R1_SB1555_overview.html]. The Oregon Department of Education's summary of
  enacted 2026 education legislation does not list it
  [SRC:ode/legislation/2026_summary-enacted-education-legislation.pdf].
